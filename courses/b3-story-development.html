Finding the story in the data

b2 got the inbound through the gate. Tonight you are at stage 3 of the publish chain, Report, and the material is a leaked spreadsheet of berth and idling times at the Harbour Ledger's port. The press release attached to it says the new terminal cuts emissions 40%. The filing underneath says 40% per container, on a projection, by 2031. Nobody fabricated anything and the story is still wrong. That gap - a real number inside a sentence that does not survive its own primary document - is the single most common way a careful newsroom publishes something false. AI empowering data journalism is one of the five forces the Reuters Institute's 2026 panel named for this year, and it is real: a model will read 4,000 rows faster than your data desk can open the file. It will also tell you what a column means, and it will be wrong, and it will sound exactly as confident either way.

Part 1 · the anatomy of a true-and-wrong number

The 40% is real and the sentence is not 6 min live

Read the press release line on its own: "the new terminal cuts emissions 40%". Every word is defensible in isolation and the number is genuinely in the filing. Now open the filing. It says 40% per container, on a projection, by 2031, against a modelled baseline. A port that moves twice as many containers can hit that figure and emit more in total. The number survived the trip to your desk. Four qualifiers did not, and the qualifiers were the story.

LiveThe four questions, asked of everything4 min▶

This is not a port story problem. It is the shape of almost every number that arrives already written into a sentence. Run all four every time, out loud if you have to:

  • Per what? Total, per unit, per head, per pound, per container. A rate and a total move in opposite directions the moment volume changes, and volume is exactly what an expansion changes.
  • Measured how? Observed, modelled, self-reported, projected, or estimated from something else entirely. "Cuts" and "is projected to cut" are different verbs doing different work.
  • By when? Now, at opening, at full build-out, by a target year. A 2031 figure in a 2026 headline is a promise being reported as an event.
  • Compared to what? Last year, a modelled do-nothing baseline, a rival port, the regulatory minimum. The baseline is where most flattering numbers are actually manufactured, and it is almost never in the release.

Note what does not catch this. A fact-check confirms the figure exists, and it does exist, so an honest desk waves it through. The check that catches it is document-opening: only the filing tells you what the figure counts. Stage 2 of the publish chain, the gate, is where this belongs, and in b2 you ran that check by name - primary document. This is what it is for. Expect the incentives to run against you: the precise sentence is longer, hedged and harder to headline, and every competitor will have run the release version. That is the story, not a reason to match them.

The one-line habit Before a number goes in a draft, write it as a full sentence with all four qualifiers in it, however ugly. If the ugly sentence and your headline disagree, the headline is wrong. b4 turns this into a headline test.
Part 2 · the boundary, drawn precisely

Where a model helps and where it invents 6 min live

A model is genuinely, unglamorously good at some of this. It will describe the shape of a dataset in seconds, spot the rows that look odd so you know where to go and check, draft the questions you should be putting to the harbour authority, explain a domain term you were too embarrassed to ask about, and translate a filing's jargon into something you can read on the bus. All of that is preparation, and preparation is exactly what stage 3 needs. What it is dangerous at is anything that ends in a figure you print.

LiveThe two lists, and why the second one is seductive4 min▶

Genuinely good at:

  • Describing a dataset's shape - rows, columns, date range, gaps, what the file appears to contain.
  • Spotting outliers so you know which rows to go and check yourself.
  • Drafting the questions you should be asking of it, and of the people who produced it.
  • Explaining a domain term, and translating a filing's jargon into plain English you then verify against the original.

Dangerous at:

  • Stating what a column means. It reads the header and pattern-matches. Headers lie, and this file has two that lie in opposite directions.
  • Computing a figure you then publish. Even when the arithmetic is right, it is arithmetic on an assumption you did not see it make.
  • Telling you a trend is significant. That is a claim about a population from a sample, and it will make it cheerfully with eleven data points.
  • Filling a gap in the data. Missing values are information. A model will smooth over the thing you were supposed to notice.
Real world

AI-empowered data journalism is one of the five themes the Reuters Institute's 2026 panel of experts named for the year, alongside audiences reaching news through AI, rising demand for verification work, agents reshaping newsrooms, and upskilling. Read those two together and you get the whole of tonight: the same technology that lets a two-person desk open a 4,000-row file is the one that raises the verification burden on everything it produces.

Build-along · everyone runs it

The leaked spreadsheet, done properly ★ 20 min live

The file is berth-and-idling-times.csv: 4,180 rows, 11 columns, eighteen months of vessel movements, leaked by somebody who did not explain it. You have no data desk tonight. Run it the way you would run an interview with a source who cannot be interrupted.

Run the describe-and-question prompt below. Read the section headed "what I cannot determine" first, before anything else. That is the honest part of the answer.

Take the model's column-meaning guesses and treat every one as unverified. Two columns in this file mean the opposite of what their names suggest, and both matter.

Pick the three rows it flagged as anomalous. Go and find out which are data-entry errors and which are the story. Do this by hand.

Open the filing, confirm one number and record the page. Then write the single sentence you could defend in a correction meeting, with all four qualifiers in it, and read it against the press release line.

Good · describe and question, compute nothingYou are helping me understand a dataset before I report on it. File: berth-and-idling-times.csv, 4,180 rows, 11 columns, 18 months. Do all of this, in this order, and nothing else: 1. SHAPE. Rows, columns, date range, missing values, duplicates. 2. COLUMNS. For each one, what it appears to contain and how confident you are. Mark anything you inferred from the header rather than from the values. 3. WHAT I CANNOT DETERMINE. Every column or figure whose meaning cannot be settled from this file alone, and what document or person would settle it. 4. ANOMALIES. Rows that look wrong or unusual, and why they caught your attention. Do not tell me what they mean. 5. QUESTIONS. Ten questions I should put to the harbour authority about how this file was produced. Rules: do not compute any figure I could publish. Do not tell me what the story is. Do not fill in missing values. If you are guessing, start the line with GUESS.
Bad · asks for a publishable conclusionHere is the leaked spreadsheet of berth and idling times at the port. What is the story in this data? Give me the top three findings with the numbers, ready to publish.
What the bad prompt actually returns Three fluent findings, each with a figure, each phrased like a standfirst. It reads like a data desk did the work. Nothing in the output tells you which column it assumed meant what, which rows it dropped, or where it interpolated. You cannot audit an answer that arrives without its assumptions attached, and you cannot defend one either.
Part 3 · the craft underneath

Reading a dataset like a reporter handbook

Five things that decide whether the story stands up. The first four are what we work through live; the last one is what saves you six months later when somebody's lawyer asks how you got the number.

LiveReading the leaked spreadsheet properly4 min▶

Column names are written by whoever built the system, for whoever maintains it, and they are not documentation. This file has two that will catch you:

  • idle_time looks like the hours a vessel sat with engines running. It is actually the hours the berth sat empty between vessels. High values are the port being quiet, not ships idling. Report it the obvious way and your emissions story is exactly backwards.
  • eta_variance looks like lateness. It is signed, and the sign convention is inverted against the industry norm: negative means late. Sum it naively and a chronically late berth averages out to punctual.

The fix is not cleverness, it is a phone call. Someone at the harbour authority knows what those columns are, and the five-minute expert call from the five checks is the cheapest thing on this page. Ask for the data dictionary. If there is not one, that absence is itself worth a paragraph.

LiveThe outlier that is an error, the outlier that is the story4 min▶

Every dataset has rows that stick out. They come in two kinds and they look identical on a chart:

  • The data-entry error. A berth with 9,999 idle hours. A vessel arriving in 1900. A negative tonnage. These are sentinel values, unit slips and fat fingers, and they are boring. Find them, exclude them, and say in the method note that you did and why.
  • The story. A berth with three times everyone else's dwell time, every Tuesday, for four months. That is not an error. That is a pattern somebody is living with, and it is the reason the file leaked.

The only way to tell them apart is to go and look at one. Pick the single most extreme row and find out what physically happened on that day. The answer is either "the system logged it wrong", which closes the question, or a phone call you were going to have to make anyway.

Never let a model decide this Asked to clean the data, it will silently drop both kinds. The 9,999 row and the Tuesday pattern get the same treatment, and the one you needed disappears without an entry in any log.
LiveSmall numbers, big percentages3 min▶

A 300% rise sounds like a crisis and is very often two incidents where there used to be one incident. The percentage is arithmetically correct and journalistically useless.

  • Always publish the counts next to the rate. "Up 300%, from one incident to four" is honest, still interesting, and impossible to misread. Any editor who cuts the counts is cutting the only context.
  • Watch the denominator moving. A rate can fall while the total rises, which is the entire trick in the 40%-per-container claim. Whenever a rate is the headline, ask what happened to the base.
  • Beware the small slice. Break 4,180 rows into eleven categories and two weekday buckets and some cells hold three rows. A model will happily compute a percentage on three rows and will not warn you.
  • A run of two is not a trend. Two consecutive rises is a coin landing the same way twice. Say "rose in each of the last two quarters", not "is rising".
LiveSourcing a dataset so a reader can check you3 min▶

This is the attribution leg of the trust line, applied to numbers. A reader who wants to check you should be able to, without emailing you:

  • Name the file and where it came from. "A spreadsheet of berth and idling times covering January 2025 to June 2026, provided to the Ledger by a person with access to the port's operations system." Vague is fine when it protects a source; unstated is not.
  • Say what you did to it. Rows excluded, how many, and why. If you dropped 14 rows with impossible timestamps, say so in one line.
  • Point at the primary document for every number that came from one. The filing, with the page. Not the release about the filing.
  • Publish derived numbers rather than the raw file when the raw file would identify the leaker. Then say that is what you did and why.
  • Say who checked it. If the harbour authority was given the figures before publication and declined to comment, that sentence is part of the evidence.
Self-studyDocumenting your method so the story survives a challenge4 min read▶

The challenge always arrives later than you expect and from someone better prepared than you remember being. A method note written on the day costs twenty minutes; reconstructing one from memory in six months is not possible.

  • Keep the original file untouched and work on a copy. If the only version you have is the one you edited, you cannot show anybody what you started with.
  • Log every transformation in plain English as you make it. "Excluded 14 rows with arrival dates before 2020. Treated blank eta_variance as missing, not zero."
  • Record which numbers came from the model and which from the filing. Every published figure needs a line saying a human confirmed it against a named document on a named date.
  • Save the exact prompts you used. They are part of the method now. A prompt that forbade computation is evidence of how you worked.
  • Write down the questions you could not answer. When the challenge comes, the fastest defence is showing you already knew the limits and published inside them. None of this is bureaucracy; it is keeping your interview notes, applied to a file instead of a person.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · The release says the terminal cuts emissions 40%. The filing says 40% per container, on a projection, by 2031. What went wrong?

This is the failure mode that passes every fact-check, because the fact is correct. A rate per container can fall while total emissions rise, and a 2031 projection is not an event. Only the filing carries the qualifiers, which is why the primary-document check exists.

2 · You ask a model what the column idle_time contains and it gives you a confident answer. What is the status of that answer?

In this file idle_time is berth idle time, not vessel idle time, so high values mean the port was quiet. A model pattern-matches on the header and will not flag its own inference unless you make it. The fix is a data dictionary or a five-minute expert call.

3 · Your analysis shows incidents up 300% year on year. What has to be in the sentence?

Publishing the rate without the base is how a two-incident change becomes a crisis headline. The same reflex catches the port claim: whenever a rate is the story, ask what the denominator did, because a per-unit fall and a total rise happily coexist.
