Capstone: one story, every format

Nine sessions ago a press release landed at the Harbour Ledger claiming the new terminal cuts emissions by 40%. The filing said something considerably more careful: 40% per container, on a projection, by 2031. Since then the story has gathered a dated trigger, a verified set of inbound, a leaked berth-times spreadsheet, a headline that survives the correction column, a 50-minute interview cut for audio, council-chamber footage cut for video, images routed through the illustration-versus-depiction gate and signed, a 1998 council promise confirmed against the original, and a distribution plan. Tonight you do not learn anything new. You assemble what you already made, score it against twelve rows, and take it through the gate one last time.

Part 0

What a capstone is for

A capstone is not a summary. It is the session where the artifacts stop being exercises and become one publishable thing with its provenance intact. Every previous session ended with something you could put on a desk. Tonight those pieces get attached to the right stage of the publish chain, checked against a scorecard, and either passed or sent back. The most useful thing you can take away is the shape of the scorecard, because it works on any story, in any newsroom, with or without any of the tools this course mentioned.

Part 1 · the whole chain, with everything attached

Assemble the story 10 min live

Walk the port expansion through all five stages of the publish chain and name the artifact each session produced. Tip: the press release and its 40% claim, dated. Verify: the inbound checked in b2, and the 1998 council promise from b8 confirmed against the original rather than against the index entry. Report: the number decomposed against the filing in b3, and the story and headline written in b4. Package: the audio cut from b5, the video cut with reviewed subtitles from b6, the images routed through the gate and signed in b7. Publish: the channel plan from b9 and the scorecard you are about to run.

LiveWalking the chain out loud, stage by stage5 min▶

Do this as a spoken pass in the room. One person names the stage, one names the artifact, and one names what would have gone wrong without it:

  • Tip. A press release with a very good number in it, and a date. Without the date, nothing downstream can be placed in time and the 1998 comparison in b8 is unusable.
  • Verify. The b2 inbound set, checked, plus the archive promise confirmed against the original document rather than the catalogue record. Two different failure modes, one gate.
  • Report. The b3 decomposition is the story: 40% is per container, on a projection, to 2031, and the release said none of that. b4 turned it into a headline that survives the correction column.
  • Package. The b5 audio cut, the b6 video with reviewed subtitles, the b7 images through the illustration-versus-depiction gate and signed. Three formats, one set of facts.
  • Publish. The b9 channel plan, derived from the published story and adding nothing to it, and the scorecard in Part 2.
Self-studyThe two places this story could still fall apart3 min read▶
  • A qualification lost in a package. The 40% figure travelled through four formats and a distribution plan. Every one of those was a chance to drop "per container, on a projection, by 2031". Check the subtitle file, the audio tease and the social caption individually, because they were each edited by someone different.
  • An artifact that was never re-checked after it changed. If the b4 story was edited after the b6 subtitles were reviewed, the subtitles now quote a version that no longer exists. Version the story, and re-run any package built on an earlier one.
Real world

This is the ordinary way good newsrooms publish wrong things. Not a fabricated quote or a deepfake, but a qualification that survived the reporting, survived the edit, and then fell off a 20-second clip that four hundred thousand people saw and eleven thousand people clicked. The correction, when it comes, is about the clip, and the clip is the version the audience remembers.

Part 2 · twelve rows, and four of them are stops

The publication scorecard 8 min live

Twelve rows. Five are the checks you have run since b2. Three are the trust line. Four are rails. The distinction matters more than anything else on this page: the eight point rows can be discussed, weighed and occasionally traded against a deadline by an editor who owns that decision. The four rails cannot. A story that fails a rail does not get a score at all. It gets fixed, and then it gets scored. There is no version of the conversation where a rail failure is offset by strength elsewhere.

#The rowType
1Reverse search run on every image and every video framePoint
2Metadata and C2PA inspected, absence treated as normal rather than as suspicionPoint
3Two genuinely independent sources, not two outlets quoting one alertPoint
4The primary document read - the filing, the dataset, the original recordingPoint
5Five minutes with someone who knows the domainPoint
6The claim is supported by evidence you actually holdPoint
7Every attribution is accurate and complete, in every formatPoint
8A correction path exists and it is fastPoint
9No generated image depicts anything realSTOP
10Every quote was heard at the audio, not read off a transcriptSTOP
11Every number was confirmed against a primary sourceSTOP
12Any AI involvement that is material has been disclosedSTOP
LiveWhy these four are rails and not just important points4 min▶
  • No generated image depicts anything real. An illustration of a concept is fine and labelled. A generated picture of the actual berth, the actual councillor or the actual protest is a fabricated record, and no amount of good reporting elsewhere makes it publishable.
  • Every quote was heard at the audio. A transcript is a draft of what was said. b5 exists because the difference between "we will not" and "we won't" and "we want to" is a resignation.
  • Every number was confirmed against a primary source. The whole port story is one number that was reported without its qualification. This is the rail that produced the story in the first place.
  • Material AI involvement is disclosed. If a reader who learned how the piece was made would feel misled, it was material. The test is the reader's reaction, not the tool's category.
How to use the scorecard without slowing the desk down Run the four rails first. They take under two minutes and they are yes-or-no. Only score the eight point rows on a story that has already cleared all four, because scoring a story you are going to send back anyway is wasted time.
Part 3 · the gate, one last time

The final gate ★ 10 min · everyone runs it

This is not a new lesson. It is the same desk you met in b1, at the top of the ladder, and it is here so that the last thing you do in this track is the thing the whole track was about. All five checks on: 10 out of 10 items handled correctly, 0 false accusations. Then switch the detector on one final time and read the wire-photo row, because that row is the argument this course would most like you to remember.

Confirm the starting state: all five checks on, 10 / 10 handled correctly and 0 false accusations. That is the bar the finished port story has to meet.

Walk the ten rows and say which session taught the check that catches each one. If you cannot name it for a row, that is the session to re-read.

Turn one check off and watch the score fall. Turn it back on. This is the fastest way to feel what a skipped check actually costs.

Switch the ⚠ AI detector on at 10/10 one last time. Read the wire-photo row in full, out loud if you are in a room.

Say the conclusion plainly: it caught nothing the desk had missed and it accused a real photograph. A tool that adds no catches and one false accusation has a negative contribution.

Switch it back off. Finish the track at 10 out of 10 with zero false accusations, which is where a newsroom should end every night.

Real world

The detector row has been the spine of this track since session one. The genuinely synthetic reader submission was already caught by file history, at 10 out of 10, before any detector was involved. The detector's only unique contribution was to spike a real wire photograph of a council vote and put a supplier under suspicion on the strength of a number nobody in the room could explain. Provenance, sourcing and primary documents are evidence. A probability is not, and it never becomes one by being expensive.

Part 4 · what happens on Monday

After the track 6 min live

Ten sessions is enough to change what you do and not enough to change what your newsroom does. The next part is yours, and it is smaller than it sounds.

LiveWhat to do on your next shift3 min▶
  • Run the four rails on whatever you publish next. Under two minutes, yes-or-no, no permission needed from anyone.
  • Reverse-search the first image that arrives. Do it before you have decided whether the story is worth it, because that is the decision the check is supposed to inform.
  • Ask one question about independence. When two sources agree, ask where each of them got it. That single question catches most matched errors.
  • Write one correction path down. Who can publish a correction on your site at 9pm on a Saturday, and how long it takes. If nobody knows, that is the finding.
  • Pick one story a week to distribute properly. The b9 discipline: derived from the published piece, adding nothing, qualification attached to every number.
LiveWhat this course deliberately did not teach3 min▶

Knowing the edges of a course is part of using it safely. Four things were left out on purpose, and one of them could get you sued:

  • Media law in any jurisdiction. Defamation, contempt and privacy are counsel's territory. Nothing in these ten sessions is legal advice, and the rights and licensing material is orientation only.
  • Building or fine-tuning models, and vendor certifications. Not because they do not matter, but because they date within a year and none of them change what happens at the gate.
  • The craft of writing itself. That is learn-ai-writing-with-phoebe. b4 taught headline standards and what may carry a byline, not how to write well.
  • Marketing and audience-growth campaigns. That is learn-ai-marketing-with-phoebe. b9 was distribution of journalism, which is a different job with a different truth constraint.
LiveThe one habit worth keeping2 min▶

If everything else fades, keep this one: the 90-second reverse search, every time.

  • It is the cheapest check on the list and it catches the most. A real picture of the wrong event is still the oldest and most common failure in the trade.
  • It needs no budget, no tool contract and nobody's approval. Which means no editor, deadline or restructure can take it away from you.
  • It works when you are tired. It is a reflex rather than a judgement, and reflexes are what survive a bad night at 22:14 with two competitors already posted.
Self-studyIf you are an editor, the a-track is the other half2 min read▶

This producer track taught what one person does at a desk. The editor track (a1-a6) covers the decisions a desk cannot make for itself:

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 45-60 min total

  • Take one real story your newsroom published in the last month and run it through all twelve rows. Score it honestly, including the rows you would rather not look at.
  • Fix every failure. A rail failure gets fixed before anything else, and if it cannot be fixed, the piece gets corrected or pulled rather than argued about.
  • Write the one habit on a sticky note and put it where you actually work: 90-second reverse search, every time.
  • Run the desk at 10/10 once more a week from now, without re-reading anything, and see whether you can still name the check that catches each row.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · A story scores 8 out of 8 on the point rows, but one image is a generated picture of the actual berth. What is its score?

The four rails are stops rather than points. They cannot be offset by strength elsewhere, and the whole reason for separating them is to remove the conversation where a deadline trades one away.

2 · At the final gate, all five checks are on and the desk reads 10 out of 10 with 0 false accusations. You switch the AI detector on. What happens, and what does it mean?

The synthetic item was already caught by file history. The detector's only unique contribution is an accusation you cannot explain, defend or publish. Adding no catches and one false accusation is a negative contribution.

3 · Which of these did this course deliberately not teach?

Four things were left out on purpose: media law, model building and vendor certifications, the craft of writing which is learn-ai-writing-with-phoebe, and marketing campaigns which is learn-ai-marketing-with-phoebe. Knowing the edges of the course is part of using it safely.
