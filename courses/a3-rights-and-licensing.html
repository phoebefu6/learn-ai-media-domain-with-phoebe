Rights, licensing and training data

This is the session where an editor-in-chief is asked a question nobody can answer with confidence yet: what happens to our journalism when it is used to train and to answer. There are four positions a publisher can take, all four are defensible, and none of them is settled law anywhere. What you can do tonight is work out which position your actual leverage supports, and price it honestly. The revenue picture helps: just over two thirds of surveyed media leaders expect AI licensing deals to bring at least some revenue within three years, and most of them see it as a minor source, while the same leaders expect search referrals to fall about 43% within three years. Read those two numbers together and the strategy writes itself. Everything on this page is orientation for a conversation with your counsel. It is not legal advice, and it is not a rule for your jurisdiction.

Part 0

Read this before anything else

The law on training data is unsettled and being actively litigated in several countries at once. Cases are running, rulings are landing and being appealed, regulators are consulting, and the position that looks obvious this quarter has been reversed elsewhere. That is not a hedge, it is the actual state of the field. So this page will not tell you whether your archive is protected, whether a crawler that ignored your terms broke a rule, or whether you can license work a freelance filed in 2014. It will tell you which questions decide the answer, so that the hour you buy from a media lawyer is spent on your position rather than on explaining the basics to you.

Part 1 · the map

Four positions, and nobody knows which is right yet 15 min live

Every publisher in the world is currently sitting in one of four positions on AI training data. Block everything. Block and litigate. License. Do nothing. Take all four seriously, because all four are being run right now by serious organisations with good lawyers. The differences are not about principle. They are about leverage, budget, and what your readers came for. A wire agency with a century of exclusive imagery and a small regional weekly with three reporters are not making the same decision, and pretending otherwise is how a small title ends up copying a strategy it cannot fund.

LiveTaking each position seriously6 min▶

Argue each one in its strongest form before you pick. If you cannot state the case for a position you are rejecting, you have not rejected it, you have just not understood it.

  • Block everything. The strongest case: your journalism is the product, your readers pay for it directly, and giving it away to systems that answer readers instead of sending them to you is straightforwardly self-harm. The honest cost: blocking is blunt. The same directives that keep a training crawler out can keep you out of the products readers are increasingly using to find news, and you may not be able to separate the two cleanly.
  • Block and litigate. The strongest case: somebody has to test this, the questions are genuinely open, and a favourable ruling is worth more than any single deal. The honest cost: litigation takes years, consumes management attention that a 40-person newsroom does not have spare, and produces nothing in the meantime. This position needs a war chest and a corpus distinctive enough to be worth fighting over.
  • License. The strongest case: revenue now, terms you negotiated, attribution and citation you specified, and a seat at the table while norms are being set. The honest cost: you are pricing something whose value nobody can calculate yet, your first deal becomes the reference point for your next one, and a bad first deal is very hard to reprice.
  • Do nothing. The strongest case, and it is real: you have no leverage, no legal budget, and every hour spent on this is an hour not spent reporting. Doing nothing deliberately is a strategy. The honest cost: when the market matures and terms are being written, you are not in the room and you have no record of ever having objected.
⚖ Saying it again, because it matters The paragraphs above describe commercial positions, not legal ones. Whether any of them is available to you, and on what terms, depends on your jurisdiction, your contracts and facts a lawyer needs to look at. This page is orientation for that conversation. It is not legal advice.
LiveLeverage: the input most publishers get wrong4 min▶

Leverage is not how important your journalism is. It is how hard you are to replace. Four things actually create it, and headcount is not one of them:

  • Exclusivity. Material nobody else holds. A court reporter's twenty years of local filings, an archive of a place nobody else covered, imagery that exists in one place.
  • Volume with structure. Not just a lot of text, but a lot of text that is clean, dated, attributed and machine-readable. A messy archive is worth far less than a smaller tidy one.
  • Recency at scale, and collective weight. Continuous fresh coverage cannot be bought once, so it prices differently from a static back catalogue. And one regional title has almost no weight alone, where forty bargaining together have a great deal, which is why the collective route in Part 3 exists.

The Harbour Ledger, our running newsroom, has one of these: thirty years of port and shipping coverage that nobody else in the region kept. That single fact should shape its whole position, and it points straight at b8, where the archive gets made findable in the first place.

Part 2 · the arithmetic

The sober revenue picture 10 min live

Here is the number that should govern how much of your week this gets. In the Reuters Institute's 2026 survey of 280 media leaders across 51 countries, just over two thirds expect AI licensing deals to bring at least some revenue within three years - and most of them see it as a minor source. Now set that against the other side of the same survey: those leaders expect search referrals to fall about 43% within three years, with a fifth expecting losses above 75%, and Google traffic to publishers already down by roughly a third in a year. A minor new revenue line does not refill a major one that is draining. Licensing is a supplement. It is not a plan.

LiveWhat the arithmetic means for how you spend the quarter4 min▶

If licensing is a minor line for most publishers and referral is a major line falling fast, then the allocation of executive attention writes itself, and it is not the allocation most newsrooms are running:

WorkWhat the numbers justify
Licensing negotiationReal, worth doing, sized as a supplement. Litigation only with a distinctive corpus and a funded appetite for years of it.
Direct audienceThe load-bearing response to a 43% expected referral fall. Newsletters, apps, habit, membership.
Rights hygieneCheap, fast, and the precondition for every other option. Contracts, terms, archive metadata.

Editor session a5 takes the audience side of this apart properly. Tonight the point is narrower: do not let a licensing conversation become the answer to a referral problem, because arithmetically it cannot be.

Real world

The uneven part is the part that decides your strategy. The referral decline is not distributed equally by size: reporting alongside the 2026 trends work put small publishers at roughly 60% of search referrals lost, medium around 47%, and large around 22%. The Harbour Ledger sits in the first bucket. That means the position it takes on licensing matters far less to its survival than what it does about readers who arrive directly, which is uncomfortable for a session about rights but true.

Self-studyPricing something nobody can value yet3 min read▶

There is no established rate card for training or retrieval rights, and anyone who shows you one has invented it. What you can do is refuse the three worst pricing mistakes. Pricing per article sounds fair and values your best investigation the same as a fixture list, where corpus-level or access-level pricing survives scrutiny better. Perpetual for a one-off fee sets terms that never expire at today's guess about a market that did not exist three years ago. Signing first and telling the newsroom after costs you internally far more than the deal earns. Whether any particular term is enforceable where you operate is, again, counsel's question and not this page's.

Part 3 · the working detail

Building your own position ◐ 22 min · work through these

Five things decide what position is actually open to you. Work through them in order, and mark anything you are unsure about for counsel rather than guessing. The output is one paragraph, dated, that you can hand to your board, your newsroom and your lawyer without changing a word between audiences.

LiveWhat you actually control, and what each thing really stops5 min▶

Publishers talk about "blocking" as though it were one action. It is four different mechanisms with four different reaches, and confusing them is how a newsroom ends up believing it is protected when it is not.

MechanismWhat it really does
robots.txt directivesA request that well-behaved crawlers honour by convention. It is signalling, not a wall. Some agents identify themselves and comply; others do not, and it does not reach anything already collected.
Terms of serviceAsserts conditions on access. Whether that binds an automated visitor, and where, is exactly the question courts are working through right now.
Paywall or registrationThe only mechanism on this list that physically withholds the text. Also the one that costs you reach, which is the trade you are actually making.
Feeds and syndicationYour own outbound pipes. Often the widest hole in a publisher's position, because a partner's terms may permit far more than yours do.
⚖ Still orientation, still not legal advice The middle column describes how these mechanisms behave in practice, not what a court in your country will hold. The enforceability of terms against automated access is contested and actively litigated, so take the table to counsel as a list of questions. And bring your robots.txt: nine times in ten, reading it aloud in the room reveals it was written by somebody who left, for a search landscape that no longer exists.
LiveCollective bargaining versus going alone4 min▶

A single 40-person title negotiating alone has approximately no leverage, and knows it, which is why so many end up in the do-nothing quadrant by default rather than by choice. Collective routes change the arithmetic:

  • What you gain. Scale that is actually worth negotiating over, shared legal cost, one set of terms instead of forty, and a reference point so nobody signs a bad first deal in isolation.
  • What you give up. Speed, and the ability to price your distinctive material differently from the pool. If your archive is genuinely exclusive, a flat collective rate may under-value it.
  • The competition question, and the practical test. Publishers coordinating on commercial terms touches competition law, the rules differ sharply by jurisdiction, and this is not a corner to improvise in. Beyond that: if your material is interchangeable with your neighbours', go collective, and if it is not, the collective route may cost you more than it earns.
LiveArchive rights, and why your back catalogue may be the asset4 min▶

Most publishers value their archive at roughly nothing, because it costs money to store and generates no traffic. In a licensing conversation it is frequently the most valuable thing in the building, for three reasons:

  • It is finished. A back catalogue is a bounded, complete corpus. That is far easier to license than a continuous feed with open-ended obligations.
  • It is often genuinely unique. Thirty years of port and shipping coverage of one harbour exists in one place. Today's council meeting is being covered by four outlets.
  • It is where your rights are murkiest. Which is the catch. Decades of freelance contributions, agency imagery, syndicated wire copy and photographs whose paperwork is in a filing cabinet somebody threw out.

Ordering an archive is a rights job before it is a technology job. Producer session b8 covers making decades of tape and text findable, and it will run into the same rights questions from the other direction. Do this inventory before you talk to anyone about licensing, because "we are not sure what we own" is the weakest position you can negotiate from.

LiveContributor and freelance contracts - the clause that decides everything5 min▶

You cannot license what you do not hold the right to license. For most publishers the binding constraint is not the law, it is their own paperwork, written years ago for a world of print syndication.

  • Find the grant clause. What exactly did the contributor grant: full assignment, an exclusive licence, a first-use licence, or something written so loosely that nobody can tell?
  • Check the scope words. "All media now known or hereafter devised" reads very differently from "publication in the newspaper and on the website". The second one is common and it is narrow.
  • Look for sub-licensing (and note who signed what and when - a 2023 standard contract and a 2011 letter of engagement are different assets, and photographers frequently sit on different terms from writers). Whether you may license onward to a third party is usually a separate permission from whether you may publish. Its absence is the single most common blocker.
  • Do not fix it retrospectively by assumption. Deciding internally that a loose clause probably covers it is not a decision you are entitled to make. It is the exact question to take to counsel.

Three contracts, twenty minutes, and you will know more about your real position than any strategy document in the building.

Self-studyWhat a deal contains besides money4 min read▶

Editors negotiating their first licensing conversation tend to focus entirely on the fee, which is the term least likely to matter in three years. The non-monetary terms are where the durable value sits:

  • Attribution and prominence. Whether your masthead is named when your journalism is used, in what form, and where exactly. A brand that appears in answers is a brand that survives the referral collapse, and a citation buried behind an expander is not the same asset as a visible credit.
  • Citation and links. Whether a reader can get from the answer to you. This is the term that decides whether the deal is a revenue line or an audience line, and audience compounds.
  • Correction propagation. When you correct a story, what happens to the version already ingested. Almost nobody asks for this. It ties straight back to the trust line from b1: the claim, the attribution, and the correction.
  • Exclusivity, term length and audit. What you are prevented from doing elsewhere and for how long, and whether you can ever find out how much of your material was used - which is what decides whether you can price the renewal.

If the cheque is small and the attribution terms are strong, that may still be the better deal. Whether any of these terms is enforceable in your jurisdiction is, once more, counsel's question and not this page's.

✅ Good prompt - inventories your own position and flags what it cannot answer 
You are helping an editor-in-chief prepare for a meeting with media counsel.
I am pasting three things below: our robots.txt, our website terms of service,
and our standard freelance contributor agreement.

Produce a rights inventory table with these columns:
  MATERIAL TYPE | WHAT WE APPEAR TO HOLD | WHERE IT SAYS SO | CONFIDENCE

Rules you must follow:
1. Quote the exact clause. No paraphrase. If a document is silent, write
   "SILENT" - do not infer.
2. Where the answer depends on jurisdiction, enforceability, or a fact not
   in these documents, write [NEEDS COUNSEL] and state the precise question
   to ask. Do NOT answer it yourself.
3. Flag any clause narrower than "onward licensing to a third party" as a
   BLOCKER row, and end with a list headed QUESTIONS FOR COUNSEL ordered by
   how much each one changes our position.

Do not tell me whether our content is legally protected. That is not what
I am asking you for.
❌ Bad prompt - asks a model to settle what courts have not 
Is our news content protected from being used to train AI models?
Can companies legally scrape our articles? Give me a clear yes or no
so I can tell the board.
Why the second one is dangerous rather than merely unhelpful It will answer. Fluently, in confident paragraphs, with the shape of a legal opinion and none of the substance, on a question that courts in several countries are actively and inconsistently deciding. Then it goes into a board paper, and a commercial decision gets made on it. A model asked a contested legal question does not say "this is unsettled", it picks a side. Ask it to inventory your documents. Ask a lawyer what they mean.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 60-90 min total

  • Inventory what you actually own versus what you merely licensed in: staff copy, freelance copy, agency imagery, wire material, user submissions, archive. One row each, and be honest about the rows you cannot fill. Run the good prompt above over your terms and contracts to start it.
  • Pull three freelance contracts from three different years and find the licensing clause in each. Note whether onward sub-licensing is permitted, refused, or simply not mentioned. Not mentioned is the most common answer and the most consequential.
  • Read your robots.txt out loud to someone, and establish who wrote it, when, and whether it still expresses anything you believe. Then write your one-paragraph position: which of the four quadrants you are in, why your leverage supports it, and what would make you move. Date it.
  • Take that paragraph to counsel, with the [NEEDS COUNSEL] list from the inventory prompt attached. That list is the agenda for the meeting, and it is why the meeting is short.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Just over two thirds of surveyed media leaders expect AI licensing to bring at least some revenue within three years. What follows from that?

The same survey has leaders expecting referrals to fall about 43% within three years. A minor new line does not refill a major draining one. Pursue the deals, size the effort honestly, and do not let a licensing conversation stand in for an audience strategy.

2 · Your robots.txt disallows every AI crawler you have heard of. What have you actually achieved?

robots.txt is a request, not a wall, and it is only one of four mechanisms. Terms assert conditions whose enforceability against automated access is exactly what courts are still deciding, a paywall is the only one that physically withholds text, and your syndication feeds may permit far more than your own terms do.

3 · A licensing partner wants your 2014-2019 archive. Which clause in your old freelance contracts decides whether the conversation can proceed at all?

Publishing rights and onward licensing rights are usually separate permissions, and the second one is frequently absent rather than refused. Silence is not consent, and deciding internally that a loose clause probably covers it is not a decision an editor is entitled to make. That is the question for counsel.
