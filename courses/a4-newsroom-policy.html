Learn AI Media with Phoebe · Editor track · Session 4 of 6

Newsroom policy and red lines

Almost every newsroom now has an AI policy, and almost none of them work. They are values statements: thoughtful, defensible, agreed by committee, and completely useless to a duty editor at 22:14 who needs to know whether the transcript she just ran has to be mentioned in the copy. A policy that cannot be applied under deadline pressure is not a policy, it is a press release you wrote to yourself. Tonight you build one your desk can actually follow, on a floor where fewer than four in ten people say they trust the news they see and where verification demand is rising rather than falling. Three tiers, a set of red lines nothing moves, an honest disclosure boundary, and a correction trigger written out to the point of being boring.

Part 0

Why the guidelines you already have are not working

Bring the document you currently call your AI policy and read the first paragraph out loud. If it contains the words "responsible", "human oversight" and "our values" before it contains a single named task, you have found the failure. Those documents are written for the board, the readers and the trade press. They are not written for the person on shift. The test is simple and it is unforgiving: hand your policy to a reporter, name a task, and see whether two different editors give the same answer in under ten seconds. If they do not, the policy is decorative, and your desk is improvising a standards position every night without knowing it.

Part 1 · the shape of a usable policy

A policy people can actually follow 13 min live

A usable policy answers exactly three questions, per task, in under ten seconds. May I use AI for this? Must I disclose it? Who signs it off? Everything else is preamble. The structure that answers all three at once is three tiers: permitted without disclosure, permitted with disclosure and named review, and prohibited. The critical design choice is that tiers sort tasks, not tools. A rule about a named product is out of date the week the next version ships. A rule about "transcribing an interview" is still correct in five years.

LiveWhat belongs in each tier, and the arguments you will have5 min▶

The tiers are easy to describe and hard to agree, because the arguments are about real cases rather than principles. Expect all three of these in the room:

  • Tier 1 - permitted, no disclosure. Transcription of your own recording, translation for comprehension rather than publication, archive and clip search, tagging, and first drafts of routine copy that a human then genuinely rewrites. The unifying property: the human output is checkable against a source the human already holds. The argument you will have is about "genuinely rewrites", and you should settle it with a threshold everyone can see rather than a feeling.
  • Tier 2 - permitted, disclose, named review. Summarising documents that inform published claims, generating headline variants, AI-assisted analysis of a dataset, subtitles and localisation that go out under your masthead. The unifying property: a machine's output is shaping something a reader will act on. The argument here is what "material" means, and Part 2 gives you a test for it.
  • Tier 3 - prohibited. The red lines. No exceptions, no seniority override, no deadline clause. If your list has an escape hatch in it, it is a Tier 2 with strong language, not a red line, and your desk will find the hatch within a month.
The default rule that saves you a hundred arguments Write this sentence into the policy: if a task is not on any list, it is Tier 2 until the standards editor says otherwise. Without it, every unlisted task quietly becomes Tier 1, because Tier 1 is what "nobody said no" feels like at 22:14.
Part 2 · the boundary and the band outside it

The red lines 9 min live

Some things must never be generated, whatever the deadline is and whoever is asking. A quote nobody said. An image presented as depicting a real event. A synthetic voice of a real person without their consent. A source. A document. A statistic with no primary source behind it. Every one of those breaks the trust line from b1 - the claim, the attribution, the correction - at the first link, and once the claim is fabricated the other two cannot repair it. Outside that boundary sits the disclosure question, and it deserves an honest answer rather than a maximalist one: readers do not want a label on every transcript, but they must be told when something material was machine-made.

LiveThe materiality test for disclosure4 min▶

Label-everything policies fail in a specific and predictable way: readers learn that the label means nothing, and then the label stops working on the day you actually need it. The useful boundary is materiality, and there is a one-sentence test for it.

Would a reasonable reader change how they read this if they knew? If yes, disclose. If no, do not clutter the page.

  • A transcript you checked against the audio. No. The reporter still verified every word, and the reader's relationship to the quote is unchanged. Subtitles proofed by a human sit here too, unless the translation itself is doing editorial work.
  • An illustration generated rather than photographed. Yes, always, and prominently. This one is not close, and b7 covers illustration versus depiction properly.
  • A dataset analysed with AI assistance where the finding is the story. Yes. The reader is entitled to know how the number was arrived at, and so is the person it is about.
  • A summary at the top of a story, machine-written. Yes. It is the part most readers will read, and it is the part with no reporter's eye on every sentence unless you put one there.

Write the label wording once, centrally, and use exactly that wording every time. Five different phrasings across five desks reads as evasion even when it is only inconsistency.

Real world

Disclosure is landing on an already-low floor, which raises the stakes both ways. Reuters Institute research puts trust at fewer than four in ten people saying they trust the news they see, with the shift toward social platforms and AI chatbots expected to push it lower. That cuts against over-labelling, which reads as a confession, and against under-labelling, which reads as concealment the first time somebody notices. Materiality is the only line that survives contact with a reader who is already sceptical.

LiveWriting red lines that hold on a bad night3 min▶

Red lines fail at the drafting stage, not at 22:14. Three rules make them hold. No conditionals - "should not normally" is an invitation, and if you cannot write "never" it belongs in Tier 2. Name the act, not the intent - "generating a quote" is checkable, while "misleading the reader" needs a judgement about someone's state of mind that nobody makes accurately on deadline. No seniority override - the moment an editor-in-chief can wave one through, every red line becomes a negotiation with the most senior person in the room.

Then say what to do instead. Every red line should carry its alternative: no synthetic voice of a real person, so use a read-in by a named journalist with a spoken credit. A prohibition with no route through it gets crossed.

Part 3 · the working detail

Bylines, corrections, training and enforcement ◐ 14 min · work through these

The tiers and the red lines are the frame. These five cards are the parts that get argued about six weeks later, when the policy meets an actual incident and everybody discovers it was silent on the thing that happened.

LiveWho gets a byline and who gets a credit line3 min▶

A byline is an assertion of responsibility, not a record of who typed. That single sentence resolves most of the argument, and the rest follows from it:

  • A byline means a person stands behind every claim in the piece. If nobody can honestly say that, the piece should not carry a byline, whatever the workflow was.
  • AI never gets a byline. It cannot be held responsible, cannot be called by a source, and cannot issue a correction. A byline on a system is a category error dressed as transparency.
  • Credit lines carry the method. "Data analysis assisted by AI tools, reviewed by [name]" belongs at the foot of the piece. Routine automated copy needs its own furniture at item level, plus a named human who reads the corrections on it.
  • Never split the difference. A half-byline, a joint byline with a tool name, or a vague "staff and technology" line is worse than either option, because it signals that responsibility is negotiable.
LiveThe correction trigger, written out4 min▶

Most correction policies say corrections will be made promptly and prominently, which decides nothing at the moment it is needed. Write the trigger out until it is boring. Five columns, no adjectives:

QuestionWhat your policy must actually say
What forces a correction?Any change to a fact, a number, a quote, an attribution, or a name. Everything else is a quiet edit. If the sentence's meaning changed for a reader who already read it, it is a correction.
Who authorises it?One named role that is always on shift, plus a documented escalation for anything involving a legal risk or a named individual. Never "the editor", who is asleep.
How fast?A clock, in hours, split by severity. A wrong number in a headline is not the same as a misspelled street name, and pretending otherwise means both move at the speed of the slower one.
Where does it appear?On the item, in the same place every time, plus wherever the wrong version travelled: the newsletter, the podcast show notes, the social cut, the syndication partner.
How is AI involvement described?Plainly and specifically. "An AI-assisted summary misstated the figure" tells a reader what happened. "A technical error occurred" is the sentence that costs you the masthead.

Test the last row against a real case before you need it. The instinct under pressure is to describe an AI failure in the passive voice, and readers read passive voice as concealment, correctly.

LiveTraining the desk in one hour3 min▶

Do not read the policy to people. Run cases. One hour, four blocks, and everybody leaves having applied it rather than heard it:

  • 0-10 · The three questions - may I, must I disclose, who signs, and nothing else from the document. 10-30 · Ten real tasks from this desk - everybody writes a tier for each privately, then you compare, and the disagreements are your remaining policy gaps.
  • 30-50 · Two hard cases and one red line - real incidents from your own newsroom if you have them, from the trade press if you do not, with the clock on. 50-60 · The correction trigger - walk one case all the way through to published wording, out loud, including the AI-involvement sentence.

Then put the one-page tier table where the work happens: the CMS, the desk wall, the shift handover doc. A policy that lives in a shared drive is a policy nobody has read since induction.

LiveWhen a staffer breaks it - the first time, and the third3 min▶

How you handle the first breach determines whether you ever hear about the second. Most newsrooms get this exactly backwards and then wonder why they only find out through readers.

  • First time, assume the policy failed. Ask what the person understood the rule to be. Usually you will find an ambiguity you wrote, a task nobody classified, or a pressure you created. Fix the document, tell the desk what changed, and name nobody.
  • Separate the breach from the harm. An undisclosed Tier 2 summary that was accurate is a process failure. A fabricated quote is a red line, and it is a different conversation with a different outcome regardless of whether anyone noticed.
  • Third time is a pattern, not an accident. Pattern breaches are a performance and standards matter, handled through your normal process, because by then the rule was clear.
  • Never punish the disclosure. The reporter who tells you they used something they should not have is doing exactly what you need. Treat that badly once and your visibility into AI use in the newsroom goes to zero permanently.
Self-studyRules that were about a tool, not a task3 min read▶

The single most reliable way to write a policy that is wrong within a year is to name a product in it. The pattern repeats:

A rule bans a named chatbot, the newsroom adopts a different one with the same capability, and complies with the letter of the policy while doing the exact thing it existed to prevent. A rule permits a named transcription service, the service adds summarisation, and a Tier 1 permission silently becomes a Tier 2 activity nobody reclassified. A rule assumes a capability limit, the limit moves, and the rule now permits something the editor who wrote it would have refused.

Write "transcribing a recording you made", not the product name. Keep a separate, dated appendix of approved tools if procurement needs one, and make it explicit that the appendix is an operational list and the tiers are the policy. Then the appendix can change monthly without reopening the standards argument.

List ten tasks. Not categories: the ten things your desk actually did most often last week. Transcribing the council hearing. Translating a shipping filing. Drafting a newsletter subject line. Write them down before you think about tiers.

Tier each one, alone, in silence. Everyone in the room writes 1, 2 or 3 next to each task without discussion. Two minutes.

Compare and mark the splits. Any task where the room disagrees goes on a separate list. Those are the tasks your current guidelines do not cover, and they are the whole value of this exercise.

Write the sign-off column, then the red lines. For each tier, one sentence: who signs, and whether it is disclosed. If you cannot name a role that is always on shift, the tier does not work at 22:14. Then write the red lines with no conditionals - if a line needs "normally" to be true, it is a Tier 2.

Add the default and the review date. Unlisted tasks are Tier 2. Review in six months. Both sentences go in the document tonight, not in the next draft.

✅ Good prompt - drafts tiers from your real task list 
You are helping a 40-person regional newsroom draft a tiered AI use policy.
Below is the list of the 10 tasks our desk performs most often, with a short
note on what each one produces and who currently checks it.

Sort every task into exactly one of three tiers, and output
TASK | TIER | ONE-LINE REASON | WHO SIGNS OFF:
  TIER 1 - permitted, no disclosure needed
  TIER 2 - permitted, disclosed if material, a named human reviews and signs
  TIER 3 - prohibited, no exceptions

Rules:
1. Judge the task, never the tool. Do not name any product.
2. If a task could sit in two tiers depending on facts I have not given you,
   output it as UNCLASSIFIED and state the exact question that would settle
   it. Do not guess.
3. Reasons must be under 15 words and must not use the words "responsible",
   "appropriate" or "our values".
4. End with any sibling task we probably also do and have not written down.

Here are the ten tasks: [paste your real list]
❌ Bad prompt - produces the values statement you already have 
Write a newsroom AI policy for us. Make sure it covers ethics,
transparency and human oversight, and keep it professional.
Why the second one produces something worse than nothing With no task list it can only generate the average of every published AI policy: fluent, agreeable, structured around principles, and unable to answer a single concrete question. Worse, it reads well enough to get approved, so the newsroom now has a policy and stops looking for one. The whole difference is the ten tasks. Everything useful in your policy comes from your own desk, not from the model.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 60-75 min total

  • Write the three tiers for your newsroom, starting from the ten most common tasks on your desk. One page, table format, sign-off column filled in for every row. Then run the ten-second test on it with two editors and a stopwatch, and fix anything that takes over thirty seconds or produces two different answers.
  • Write the correction trigger in full: what forces a correction, who authorises it, how fast by severity, where it appears including every place the wrong version travelled, and how AI involvement is described in plain words.
  • Test both against the last three things your desk published. For each piece, name the tier of every task that went into it and whether the disclosure you gave matches what the policy now requires. Expect at least one mismatch, and treat it as the policy's first useful output.
  • Put the tier table where the work happens - the CMS, the desk, the handover doc - and put a six-month review date in the document itself.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your AI policy opens with a paragraph about responsibility, transparency and human oversight. What is wrong with it?

A values statement is written for the board and the trade press. A policy is written for the person on shift. Run the ten-second test: two editors, one named task. If they disagree or take thirty seconds, the document is decorative and your desk is improvising a standards position every night.

2 · A reporter transcribes a council hearing with a tool and checks every quote against the audio. A picture editor generates an illustration of the proposed terminal. Which needs disclosure?

The test is materiality: would a reasonable reader read this differently if they knew? Labelling everything teaches readers that the label means nothing, so it stops working on the day you need it. Under-labelling reads as concealment the first time somebody notices.

3 · A reporter comes to you and admits she used an AI summary in a piece without disclosing it. The summary was accurate. What is the right first move?

First breaches almost always expose an ambiguity you wrote or a task nobody classified. Punish the person who volunteered it and your visibility into AI use across the newsroom drops to zero permanently, which is a far bigger standards problem than one undisclosed summary. A fabricated quote would be a different conversation, because that is a red line.
