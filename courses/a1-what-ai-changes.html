Learn AI Media with Phoebe · Editor track · Session 1 of 6

What AI actually changes in a media org

Not the sales pitch and not the panic. The Reuters Institute's 2026 research asked 280 media leaders across 51 countries what is actually happening, and five themes came back every time: audiences reaching news through AI, rising demand for verification work, automation and agents reshaping newsrooms, upskilling and AI infrastructure, and AI empowering data journalism. Underneath those five sits a vice. Search referrals are expected to fall about 43% within three years, Google traffic to publishers already fell by roughly a third in a year, and fewer than four in ten people say they trust the news they see. Fewer people, less money, more checking, lower trust. Tonight we take the five forces one at a time for a 40-person newsroom rather than a national title, and we get honest about what got cheaper, what got more expensive, and what your masthead is actually for.

Part 1 · the research, translated to your floor

The five forces 7 min live

The Reuters Institute surveyed 280 media leaders across 51 countries for its 2026 trends work, and the expert forecasts inside it name five recurring themes. They are usually presented as industry weather. They are not weather. Each one lands on a specific Tuesday, changes a specific piece of work, and consumes a specific resource you do not have spare. Here they are, sized for 40 people rather than 4,000.

LiveThe five forces, one at a time, at your size5 min▶

Take them in order, and each time ask the same question: what does this change on a normal Tuesday at the Harbour Ledger?

  • Audiences reaching news through AI. A national title loses a slice of referral and keeps a brand people search for by name. You lose the slice and the name recognition at the same time, because most of your readers never knew the masthead was the source. The work is turning summarised readers into ones who arrive on purpose.
  • Rising demand for verification work. This is the one nobody budgeted for. Generating plausible material got cheap, checking it did not, and the checking lands on the same handful of people who were already doing everything else. One disputed clip can consume an evening shift.
  • Automation and agents reshaping newsrooms. Genuinely useful for the furniture: results, listings, weather, routine councils. The trap is thinking the saving is the drafting time. The saving is drafting time minus review time, and review time is senior time.
  • Upskilling and AI infrastructure. At 4,000 people you build a team. At 40 you send two people on a course and hope it spreads, which it does not by itself. The realistic unit is a named person with protected hours, not a programme.
  • AI empowering data journalism. The genuinely good news. The port expansion filing is 180 pages and nobody was ever going to read it. Now someone can, in a day, and the story that comes out of it is one a competitor cannot copy.
Real world

Four of the five forces cost you something, and one of them pays. That is not pessimism, it is the shape of the research. Themes one to four all consume scarce attention or money. Theme five is the only one that reliably produces stories, and it is the one most small newsrooms have not started on because it looks like a project rather than a shift. If you take one decision out of tonight, make it that one: name the document nobody has read.

Part 2 · the shape of the problem

The vice 6 min live

Two jaws, closing at the same time. On the money side, news executives expect search referrals to fall about 43% within three years and a fifth of them expect losses above 75%, while Google traffic to publishers already fell by roughly a third in the year to November. On the work side, verification demand is rising and trust is already under four in ten. Fewer people, less money, more checking, lower trust. Between the jaws sits the masthead, and it is the only asset the squeeze cannot take from you, which is precisely why the trust line is a business decision and not an ethics slide.

LiveWhy the masthead is the asset, in plain commercial terms4 min▶

Strip the sentiment out and the argument still stands. When plausible text, audio and images become effectively free, the scarce thing is not content. The scarce thing is a name that will say "this is true, and here is how we know" and accept the cost of being wrong in public.

  • Everything else is being commoditised. Aggregation, summarisation and reformatting are now table stakes. Nobody pays a premium for them and nobody ever will again.
  • The trust line is the product. The claim, the attribution and the correction are the three things a reader must know came from a person. Every one of them is a thing an automated pipeline cannot supply, which is exactly why they retain value.
  • Trust under four in ten is a floor, not a verdict. It is also an opportunity: in a market where four in ten trust the news, being visibly the one that shows its method is a differentiator rather than a hygiene factor.
  • The correction policy is a commercial document. a2 and a4 treat it that way. If it does not exist before you need it, you will write it in the worst hour of your year.
Self-studyThe uneven size effect - why this is not the same problem for everyone3 min read▶

The referral collapse is not distributed evenly, and the difference is not a rounding error. In the reported data the hit scales inversely with size:

Publisher sizeSearch referral lossWhat that means
Small (1,000-10,000 daily page views)about 60%Existential. The acquisition channel is gone, not reduced.
Mediumabout 47%Severe. Forces a direct-audience strategy this year.
Largeabout 22%A margin problem. Painful, survivable, absorbed by brand traffic.

Read the top row twice. A small publisher losing about 60% of search referrals is not losing traffic, it is losing the mechanism by which strangers ever discovered it existed. That is why the small-publisher answer is never "optimise for the new search" and always "build a channel where the reader arrives on purpose": newsletter, podcast, app, event, membership. The Harbour Ledger's daily newsletter is not a nice extra, it is the only channel nobody can turn off. It also explains why a national title's AI strategy is bad advice for you: they are managing a margin, you are choosing a survival channel. a5 works through what replaces referral in detail.

Part 3 · the ledger

What it costs and what it buys 20 min · live and self-study

Every AI conversation in a newsroom goes wrong in the same place: someone lists what got cheaper and nobody lists what got more expensive, so the business case looks like free money. Here is both columns, and then the honest list of things AI cannot do for you at all.

LiveWhat genuinely got cheaper4 min▶

These are real, they are already deployable in a 40-person newsroom, and none of them require a project:

  • Monitoring. Watching council agendas, filings, court lists and 30 local social accounts used to be somebody's morning. It is now a standing job that surfaces candidates for a human to judge.
  • Translation. A regional newsroom with a migrant community can now read the community's own sources instead of waiting for someone to translate them. This changes what you can cover, not just how fast.
  • Transcription. The single largest unglamorous saving in journalism. Interviews, council meetings, the weekly podcast. Hours per week, freed, with no editorial judgement transferred.
  • Archive search and routine first drafts. Decades of tape and text that were kept and unreachable, now searchable (b8 covers it, rights questions included), plus the furniture: results, listings, weather, routine council outcomes.

Notice what these have in common: not one of them asserts anything. They prepare, gather, convert and retrieve. The b1 rule holds at the org level too - automate the preparation, never the assertion.

LiveWhat got more expensive, and who is paying for it4 min▶

This is the column missing from every vendor deck:

  • Verification. More inbound, more of it plausible, and the same five checks to run. The cost is senior attention at the worst time of day.
  • Provenance. Keeping a chain of custody on your own material, signing what you publish, and knowing where every image came from. Fewer than 1% of news images published globally carry C2PA metadata today, so this is mostly work you do alone for now. a2 covers it properly.
  • Corrections. Not just the correction itself but the process around it: who authorises, how fast, where it appears, and the standing cost of being the outlet that publishes them visibly.
  • Audience acquisition. This is the big one. The channel that brought you strangers for free is closing, and every replacement (newsletter, podcast, app, events) costs real money and real staff time per reader acquired.
The board sentence "AI reduced our production cost per item and increased our cost per verified item and per acquired reader. The first number is the one vendors quote. The other two are the ones that decide whether we exist in three years."
Self-studyThe honest list - what AI cannot do in a newsroom3 min read▶

Not "cannot do yet". Cannot do, because the thing being asked for is not an information-processing task:

  • It cannot decide something is true. It can retrieve, compare and summarise. Deciding to assert a claim in public is an act with liability attached, and liability does not attach to a tool.
  • It cannot be accountable. A correction requires someone whose name and standing are on the line. That is the whole mechanism.
  • It cannot make a source trust you, and it cannot be in the room. Sources talk to people who protected the last person who talked to them, and the council meeting where the tone changed was not written down. The Harbour Ledger's edge on the port story is that a reporter has been going to those meetings for six years.
  • It cannot judge whether a story is worth doing. It has no stake in the community, and news judgement is a claim about what a specific place should be paying attention to.
  • It cannot carry a byline. a4 makes this a written policy rather than an assumption.
Self-studySix questions to ask any vendor selling you a newsroom AI tool4 min read▶

Take these into the demo. Ask them in this order, and write down the answers, because most of the value is in which question the room goes quiet on. A vendor who answers all six well is rare and worth keeping. One who answers question 1 with a demo and question 3 with "it is just a productivity tool" has told you what you needed to know.

✅ Ask these six, in this order 
1. When this tool is wrong, how do we find out - and does the failure
   look obvious or plausible? Show me a failure, not a demo.
2. What exactly happens to our material? Where is it stored, who else
   can see it, and is it used to train anything? Point at the clause.
3. Does this touch the assertion or only the preparation? If it drafts
   anything a reader will read as fact, say so now.
4. What does it cost us in review time per item, not just licence fee
   per seat? Give me the number your existing customers report.
5. Can we leave? What do we take with us - archive, embeddings,
   transcripts, provenance records - and in what format?
6. Who at your company answers the phone at 22:00 when this is wrong
   on our front page & we are 20 minutes from a correction deadline?

Before the demo, write down the specific job you are hiring the tool for, in one sentence, in your own words.

During the demo, ask the six questions in order. Note which one changes the room's temperature.

After the demo, map the tool onto the publish chain. If it lands on stage 2, it needs a far higher bar, and the review cost per item decides the case before the licence cost does.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your search referrals fall the way the 2026 forecasts describe. Which part of the operation is hit first?

The squeeze is asymmetric. Production got cheaper while acquisition got dramatically more expensive, and for the smallest publishers the loss is around 60%, which is not a reduction in traffic but the removal of the mechanism by which strangers discovered you at all.

2 · A vendor promises a newsroom AI tool that halves your verification workload. What is the first question?

Anything landing on stage 2 of the publish chain needs a far higher bar than anything else, because the gate is what protects the other four stages. Price and integration matter, but a plausible-looking failure inside the gate is the thing that ends up as a correction with your name on it.

3 · Why is the trust line a business decision rather than an ethics slide?

When plausible text, audio and images become effectively free, aggregation and reformatting stop carrying value. What remains scarce is a name that will assert something is true, show how it knows, and accept the cost of being wrong in public. That is a commercial asset, and it is the one thing on the balance sheet that appreciates as everything else gets cheaper.
