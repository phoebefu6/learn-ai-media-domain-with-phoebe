Writing and headlines at newsroom standard

You verified the inbound in b2 and found the story in the leaked spreadsheet in b3. Tonight you write it, and this is where the Harbour Ledger's masthead is actually spent. A model is a good line editor and a bad reporter, and the difference is not obvious in the output: fluent copy that invented a quote reads exactly like fluent copy that did not. So the boundary has to be drawn before you start, not spotted afterwards. Two rules do most of the work. AI may reshape material you already reported; it may never add material you did not. And the headline is the sentence most likely to overclaim, because a model optimises for the shape of a headline rather than for what the story supports - which is how "port expansion to cut emissions 40%" gets written over a filing that says per container, on a projection, by 2031.

Part 1 · the line through the copy

What AI may draft and what carries your byline 7 min live

The safe uses have one thing in common: every one of them reshapes material you already reported. A structure pulled from your own notes. A plain-English version of a technical paragraph you already understand. A shorter cut of a sentence you already wrote. A first pass at routine copy - the weather, the fixture list, the results table - that you then rewrite. Nothing new enters the story. The moment something new enters, you are outside the line, and the byline does not move with it.

LiveThe four things a model may never draft4 min▶

Each of these is a place where new material enters the story, and each maps onto a leg of the trust line:

  • A quote. Quotation marks are a claim that these are the words a person said. A model will produce something the person plausibly might have said, which is the definition of a fabricated quote no matter how reasonable it sounds. Paraphrase belongs to you too, but the marks are absolute.
  • A fact you have not checked. Fluent copy fills gaps by default. Ask it to write around a hole in your reporting and it will close the hole, smoothly, with something that was never verified at stage 2.
  • A characterisation of what someone believes. "The council is determined to press ahead" is an assertion about a state of mind. Unless someone said it, you invented their position, and they will notice.
  • An ending that asserts a conclusion. Models are trained to land a piece. The last paragraph is where an unsupported conclusion arrives dressed as a summary, and it is the paragraph subs read least carefully.

The claim and the attribution are two of the three legs of the trust line from b1, and both live in this list; the third leg, the correction, is what you spend when you get it wrong. One thing on the safe side is worth naming now, because most newsrooms do it badly: house style. Style enforcement is mechanical, exhausting and done poorly by humans at 22:40, and it is a genuinely good use of a model - on one condition, that you hand it your actual style guide rather than asking it to guess. "Use house style" gets you an average of every guide it has ever seen, which is nobody's. Part 3 has the full method.

The test that fits on a sticky note Point at any sentence and ask: is this material I reported, reshaped - or material that appeared? Reshaped is fine. Appeared is yours to check or cut.
Part 2 · where overclaiming happens

The headline problem 7 min live

Ask a model for headlines and you get twenty. They are all well-formed, most are punchy, and roughly three are accurate. This is not a defect you can prompt away, because it is doing what it was asked: producing things shaped like headlines. Headline shape rewards the definite verb, the round number and the dropped qualifier, and those are precisely the four things b3 told you never to drop. A hedged story with a confident headline is the most common overclaim in publishing, and it is the one that costs you a correction on the piece you were proudest of.

LiveThe correction-column test, applied word by word4 min▶

Take headline A - "Port expansion to cut emissions 40%" - and test it the way b3 taught you to test a number. Every word has to be defensible on its own:

  • "to cut" - a definite future. The filing says projected. That verb alone is the overclaim, and it is the word a model reaches for because headlines like definite verbs.
  • "emissions" - unqualified, so a reader hears total emissions. The filing means per container, and a port moving more containers can hit the target while emitting more. Only "40%" survives untouched, which is the trap: the checkable part checks out.
  • Nothing at all about 2031, or about the modelled baseline the comparison is made against. Two of the four qualifiers do not appear even in shadow.

Headline C - "Port's 40% emissions cut is per container, filing shows" - keeps the number, keeps the pull, adds the qualifier that changes the meaning, and reads like a story your competitors do not have. Because they do not: they ran A.

Real world

Trust is the floor you are writing on. Fewer than four in ten people report trusting the news they see, and the Reuters Institute expects the shift toward social platforms and AI chatbots to push that lower. An overclaimed headline is not a small win with a small risk attached. It is a withdrawal from the one account the search-referral collapse cannot touch.

Self-studyWhy the model overclaims, structurally3 min read▶

It helps to know this is not carelessness you can scold out of it:

  • It is optimising for form, not for support. Asked for a headline, it produces the most headline-shaped string available. Headline shape has a house style of its own: short, active, definite, numerate.
  • Hedges are the first thing cut for length. "Projected", "per container", "by 2031" are exactly the words that lose a fight with a character count, and the model has learned that fight from millions of examples.
  • It cannot see your evidence. It has your draft, not your notes, not the filing, and not the phone call where the spokesperson would not confirm the baseline. It does not know which parts are load-bearing.
  • Volume disguises the problem. Twenty options feel like a choice. In practice they cluster around the same overclaim, phrased twenty ways, and the best of twenty near-identical wrong answers is still wrong.
  • So use it as a generator and yourself as the gate, which is the shape of everything else in this track. Ask for twenty, discard on accuracy first, and only then argue about which survivor sings.
Build-along · everyone runs it

Two prompts, and the difference between them ★ 12 min live

Same story, same model, ten seconds apart. The first prompt hands over material and asks for edits you can audit. The second asks for a story and gets one, including the parts nobody reported.

Paste the relevant section of your real style guide, not a description of it, and your own draft underneath.

Run the flagged line-edit prompt. Every change arrives with a reason and a rule reference you can check.

Accept only the changes that remove a habit. Reject anything that changes meaning, tightens a hedge, or adds a clause you did not write.

Now run the bad prompt on the same story and read what it invented: a quote nobody gave, a motive nobody stated, an ending nobody reported. Then write four headlines yourself, plot them on accuracy against pull, and publish from above the correction line.

Good · flagged line edits, no new factsYou are line-editing a news story for the Harbour Ledger. Below are our style guide, pasted verbatim, and my own reported draft. STYLE GUIDE: <paste the actual relevant sections here> MY DRAFT: <paste your own draft here> Do this: - Suggest line edits for style-guide compliance, clarity and length. Nothing else. - Output a numbered list of changes. For each: the original text, the proposed text, and the reason, quoting the style rule where one applies. - Do NOT return a rewritten article. - Do NOT introduce any fact, name, figure, quote, motive or conclusion that is not already in my draft. - Do NOT remove or soften a hedge. If a sentence reads cautious, assume the caution is load-bearing and say so instead. - Flag separately any sentence that asserts more than the rest of the draft supports.
Bad · asks it to be the reporterWrite an engaging news story about the port expansion and its 40% emissions cut. Around 600 words, punchy intro, include some quotes, end on a strong line.
What the bad prompt actually returns 600 competent words containing a council spokesperson who does not exist saying something reasonable, an environmental group characterised as "cautiously welcoming", the 40% presented as a total cut happening now, and a closing line asserting the expansion is a turning point for the region. Not one of those came from reporting. All of them are publishable-looking, and the quote alone would end a career.
Part 3 · the craft underneath

The rest of the page, done properly handbook

The headline is not the only place meaning leaks. Four more surfaces where a model helps or hurts, and one card on the thing nobody warns you about: what smoothing does to a voice.

LiveHouse style as an AI job, done properly4 min▶
  • Extract the mechanical rules into one pasteable block - numerals, dates, titles, abbreviations, the words you ban. Fifty lines will cover 90% of what subs actually correct.
  • Keep the judgement rules out of it. "Avoid euphemism" is not a rule a model can apply without making editorial calls. Leave those with the desk.
  • Run it as the last pass, never the first, and never let it touch a quote. Quotes are exempt from house style by definition, and a tidy-up is exactly what a model will do to one without mentioning it.
LiveThe subhead and the standfirst3 min▶

The standfirst is where the qualifier the headline could not carry goes. Treat it as part of the claim, not as decoration:

  • If the headline is tight, the standfirst carries the hedge. "The figure is per container and projected to 2031, according to the filing." That sentence buys the headline its confidence honestly.
  • Never let it repeat the headline in longer words. A model will do this by default, because the two look the same to it. The standfirst should add the thing the headline had to drop.
  • Check the pair together. Headline plus standfirst is what a reader actually receives, and it is what a complaint will quote. A headline that overclaims and a standfirst that quietly corrects it is not a fix, it is two errors.
LiveWriting for a reader who arrived from an AI answer4 min▶

Audiences reaching news through AI is one of the five themes the Reuters Institute's 2026 panel named for the year, and it changes who lands on your page. This reader has a summary, no context, possibly a garbled version of your own story, and no idea which outlet they are on.

  • Say what this is, early. One clause naming the story, the place and the date. Assume nothing carried over from wherever they came from.
  • Restate the claim in full at least once, qualifiers included, in a sentence that survives being quoted alone. It probably will be.
  • Put your evidence where a skimmer hits it. If your differentiator is that you read the filing, that belongs high, not in paragraph fourteen.
  • Do not write for the summariser. Copy engineered to be extracted reads like copy engineered to be extracted, and b9 deals with distribution properly.
LiveThe correction-proof sentence3 min▶
  • One sentence per story you could read aloud in a complaints meeting. Attribute inside it, not in the paragraph after. "According to the environmental filing lodged in March" is doing structural work, not adding words.
  • Use the verb the evidence supports. Says, projects, claims, estimates, confirmed. "Will" needs a level of proof you almost never have.
  • Read it as your opponent. If the council's press office could quote it back at you to show you were unfair, rewrite. If they could quote it to show you were exact, you are done.
Where this sentence goes High in the piece, in the standfirst if it fits, and verbatim in the social cut. It is the version you want quoted when someone screenshots you.
Self-studyVoice, and why smoothed copy reads competent and forgettable4 min read▶

Run a piece through a model for polish and something specific happens. The sentences get more even. The odd rhythm goes. The one strange word you chose on purpose becomes a normal word. Nothing is wrong, and nothing is yours.

  • Smoothing removes variance, and voice is variance. A model moves every sentence toward the middle of what a sentence like that usually looks like. The middle is where nobody remembers anything.
  • The first casualty is the specific detail. "The spokesperson checked her phone twice before answering" becomes "the spokesperson declined to elaborate". The second is shorter and it is not reporting.
  • Put the reporting back in. The cure for forgettable copy is almost never a better sentence, it is a better observed fact. If a paragraph reads flat, ask what you saw that is not in it.

Keep a before file and read your draft against the polished version once a week, so you notice what got smoothed away. The practical rule: use a model to cut, rarely to rewrite. Cutting cannot invent, smooth or characterise. It is the safest edit there is, and it is the one most drafts actually need.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 35-45 min total

  • Take your own last published piece and run it through the flagged line-edit prompt, with your real style guide pasted in.
  • Accept only the changes that remove one of your worst habits. Reject every change that alters meaning, softens a hedge, or adds a clause. Count both piles.
  • Write four headlines for that piece by hand, rate each on accuracy and on pull, and mark which sit below the correction line. Then test the winner word by word: if one word could not survive the correction column, it is not the winner.
  • Write the correction-proof sentence for a story you are working on now, put it in the standfirst, then read your draft next to a polished version and put back one thing the smoothing took out.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Which of these is inside the line - something a model may draft for a story carrying your byline?

Every safe use reshapes material you already reported. Shortening your own sentence adds nothing new. A concluding paragraph asserts something the reporting may not support, and a plausible quote is a fabricated quote however reasonable it sounds.

2 · A model returns twenty headlines for your port story. What is the first thing you do with them?

It optimises for headline shape, and headline shape rewards the definite verb and the dropped qualifier. Twenty options usually cluster around the same overclaim, so widening the pool does not help. Filter on what the story supports, then choose.

3 · You want a model to enforce house style on a finished draft. What makes it a safe job rather than a risky one?

"Standard style" is an average of every guide it has seen, and a clean rewrite hides what moved. Flagged changes let a sub accept or reject each one, and the explicit no-new-facts rule blocks the clarifying clause that style passes love to insert. Quotes stay exempt.
