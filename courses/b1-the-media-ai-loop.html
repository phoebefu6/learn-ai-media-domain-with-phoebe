Part 1 · the spine of the track

The publish chain, and the gate in the middle of it 7 min live

Everything you publish passes through five stages: Tip (something arrives), Verify (is it true, and how would you know), Report (what is the story), Package (text, audio, video, images), Publish (and stand behind it). AI is now genuinely useful in four of the five. The one it cannot do for you is the gate, and the gate is the only stage that protects the other four.

LiveThe five checks4 min▶

The gate is not a feeling. It is five specific things, each of which catches a different kind of wrong, and each of which costs a known number of minutes:

  • Reverse search - the oldest failure in the book: a real picture of the wrong event. Ninety seconds, and it catches more than anything else on this list.
  • Metadata and C2PA - file history, capture data, and Content Credentials where they exist. Note the reality: fewer than 1% of news images and videos published globally carry C2PA metadata today, so absence proves nothing. Presence is useful, absence is normal.
  • Two sources - genuinely independent ones. Two outlets quoting the same wire alert is one source wearing two hats, and it is how most matched errors happen.
  • Primary document - the filing, the dataset, the transcript, the original recording. Not the press release about it, and not a summary of it.
  • Expert call - five minutes with someone who knows the domain. The cheapest check on the list and the first one cut when the clock is running.

There is a sixth toggle in tonight's desk - an AI detector. It is not a check. You will find out why in Demo 2, and get the full treatment in b2 and b7.

Real world

The verification workload is going up, not down. The Reuters Institute's 2026 survey of 280 media leaders across 51 countries names rising demand for verification work as one of the five defining forces of the year, alongside audiences reaching news through AI, agents reshaping newsrooms, upskilling, and AI-empowered data journalism. The generation of plausible material got cheap. Checking it did not.

Part 2 · the squeeze, and the three things that stay human

The trust line 6 min live

Here is the shape of the problem, and it is a vice rather than a trend. On one side the money is being withdrawn: leaders expect search referrals down about 43% in three years, a fifth of them expect losses above 75%, and publisher traffic from Google already fell by roughly a third in a year - by about 60% for the smallest publishers. On the other side the verification burden rises and trust is already under four in ten. Fewer people, less money, more checking, lower trust. Three things have to stay unmistakably human or the masthead stops meaning anything, and the masthead is the only asset the squeeze cannot take.

LiveProvenance: what C2PA actually gives you, and what it does not4 min▶

Content Credentials attach a signed history to a file: what device or tool made it, what edits were applied, by whom. Where present it is genuinely strong evidence. The reality check is adoption.

  • Fewer than 1% of news images and videos published globally carry C2PA metadata today. Pilot adoption is growing at agencies and broadcasters including the BBC, which is real progress from a very small base.
  • So absence is not evidence. The overwhelming majority of genuine photographs you handle will have no credentials at all. Treating "no C2PA" as suspicious would mean disbelieving almost everything true.
  • Presence is asymmetric and useful. A valid credential chain tells you a great deal quickly. A missing one tells you to fall back on the other four checks.
  • Your own output matters too. Signing what you publish is how the base rate improves, and b7 covers doing it on your own images.
Self-studyWhy detection tools are not on the list of checks3 min read▶

The intuitive move when synthetic media floods in is to buy a detector. It fails for a structural reason, not a quality reason:

  • A detector answers the wrong question. It guesses whether pixels were generated. You need to know where a file came from and what happened to it - a provenance question, not a pixel question.
  • Your pipeline destroys the signal. By the time an image reaches your desk it has been resized, recompressed and colour-corrected by several systems. Those steps look like generation to a detector.
  • A probability is not publishable. "Our tool scored it 87%" is not a sentence you can put in a correction, defend to a supplier, or say to a contributor you have just accused.
  • The asymmetry is brutal. A false negative costs you one story. A false accusation against a real photographer or a genuine contributor costs you a relationship and, eventually, the masthead.

Tonight's sixth toggle behaves the way this behaves. Turn it on at the top of the ladder and watch the false-accusation counter, not the score.

Demo 2 of 2

The 10-item desk - your course-long benchmark ★ 12 min · explore

One night of inbound: wire photos, a flood clip, a leaked spreadsheet, a spliced audio file, a satellite image, a press release with a very good number in it. Start with every check off and climb. The ladder is 1 → 2 → 4 → 6 → 8 → 10, and each session in this track turns on the check that unlocks the next rung. Then, at 10 out of 10, turn on the detector and watch the second counter.

All checks off: 1/10. Only the wire photo survives, and only because it was already true.

+ Reverse search: 2/10. + Metadata and C2PA: 4/10. Watch which items flip and read the failure text on the rest.

+ Two sources: 6/10. + Primary document: 8/10. The two document items are where most real-world corrections come from.

+ Expert call: 10/10. Five minutes each, and it was the cheapest check on the list.

Now, at 10/10, turn on ⚠ AI detector. The score drops to 9 and a second number appears. Read the wire-photo row.

Real world

That detector result is the point of the toggle, not a gimmick. At 10 out of 10 the desk had already caught everything, including the genuinely synthetic reader submission, using file history. The detector's only unique contribution was to flag a real wire photograph and put a supplier under suspicion on the strength of a number nobody in the room could explain. A tool that adds false accusations without adding catches has a negative contribution, however good it feels to have bought something.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 25-35 min total

  • Run the full ladder on the desk - 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 - until you can predict which items flip before clicking. Then run the detector toggle once and write one sentence about what it did.
  • Take the last three things you published. For each, name which of the five checks you actually ran. Most people find they ran one.
  • Time yourself doing a reverse image search on a real inbound photo. If it takes more than two minutes, your workflow is the problem, not your standards.
  • Look up whether your own published images carry any provenance metadata. If they do not, you are part of the under-1%, and b7 fixes it.
  • Draw the publish chain from memory and mark where your newsroom's last correction actually originated. It is almost always stage 2.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · An image arrives with no C2PA metadata at all. What does that tell you?

Provenance is asymmetric. A valid credential chain is strong evidence; a missing one is the overwhelming default and simply sends you back to the other four checks. Treating absence as suspicion would mean disbelieving almost every true photograph you handle.

2 · Three outlets are reporting a minister has resigned. Which check applies?

Matched errors happen when independence is assumed rather than checked. If all three trace back to the same alert, you have one origin and no confirmation, and when it collapses you collapse with it.

3 · At 10 out of 10 on the desk, switching on the AI detector drops the score and adds a false accusation. Why is that the honest result?

The genuinely synthetic item was already caught by file history. The detector added no catch and one accusation against a real wire photograph - and "our tool scored it 87%" is not a sentence you can publish, defend, or apologise with.
