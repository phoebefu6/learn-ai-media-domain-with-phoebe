Part 1 · covers the Reuters Institute 2026 verification finding

The ladder, and what each rung costs 6 min live

The five checks are not equal and they are not interchangeable. Each catches a different family of wrong, and each has a real price in minutes on a night when you do not have minutes. The cheapest check on the list is also the one that gets cut first, which is worth sitting with for a moment.

LiveWhat each check costs and what it catches4 min▶

Print this table. It is the whole session.

CheckCostCatchesBlind to
Reverse searchabout 90 secondsReal material of the wrong event, wrong place or wrong yearAnything never published before
Metadata + C2PA2-3 minutesEdit chains, generator signatures, missing capture dataAnything stripped in transit, which is most things
Two sources10-30 minutesSingle-origin claims wearing three outlets as a disguiseA genuinely wrong consensus
Primary document15-45 minutesA real number inside a sentence that does not survive the filingWhat the document itself gets wrong
Expert call5 minutesDomain-specific impossibility: a splice, a shadow, a unit that makes no senseWhatever your expert has not seen either

Two things jump out of that table. The cheapest checks catch the most common failures - reverse search is 90 seconds and it catches the single most frequent thing that goes wrong. And the expert call is five minutes, which makes it the best value on the list by a distance, and it is nearly always the one skipped, because it requires admitting out loud that you do not know something.

Real world

This workload is going up, not down. The Reuters Institute's 2026 survey of 280 media leaders across 51 countries names rising demand for verification as one of the five defining forces of the year. Generating plausible material got close to free. Checking it costs exactly what it always did, which means verification is now the expensive half of the job and has to be resourced like it.

LiveRouting: what to run on what3 min▶

You will rarely run all five on one item. Route by what the item IS:

  • A photo or video from social - reverse search first, always, then metadata. Ninety seconds gets you most of the way.
  • A claim that other outlets are already carrying - two independent sources. Trace every one back to its origin before you match it.
  • A number in a press release - primary document. Always. The number is usually real and the sentence around it usually is not.
  • A dataset or a leak - primary document plus an expert, because the field definitions are where you will be wrong.
  • Audio or imagery where a technical judgment is needed - expert call. Five minutes with someone who does this professionally beats an hour of you squinting.
  • Anything from a contributor you have never worked with - metadata plus reverse search, and a polite question about the original file.

The routing habit is the actual skill. Anyone can run five checks with unlimited time. Working out which one applies in four seconds at 22:14 is the job.

Part 2 · the toggle that is not a check

Why detection tools are not on the list 6 min live

When synthetic media floods in, the intuitive purchase is a detector. It fails for structural reasons rather than quality reasons, and the failure gets worse as your pipeline gets more professional - which is the opposite of what you want from a safety tool.

Self-studyThe four structural reasons, in order3 min read▶
  • It answers the wrong question. Whether pixels were generated is not what you need to know. You need to know where the file came from and what happened to it - a provenance question with checkable answers.
  • Your pipeline destroys the evidence it reads. By the time an image reaches your desk it has been resized, recompressed and colour-corrected by several systems, and every one of those steps looks like generation. The better your production chain, the worse the detector performs.
  • A probability is not publishable. You cannot write "our tool scored it 87%" in a correction, say it to a supplier, or use it to explain to a freelancer why you spiked their picture.
  • The asymmetry is brutal. A miss costs you one story. A false accusation against a real photographer costs a relationship, a reputation, and eventually the thing the whole newsroom runs on.

And the reason to teach it as a toggle rather than a paragraph: everyone agrees with the paragraph and buys the tool anyway. Watching it flag a genuine wire photo at 10 out of 10 is harder to argue with.

Self-studyWhat to do instead when you genuinely cannot tell2 min read▶

Sometimes all five checks come back inconclusive. That is a real situation and it has a real answer:

  • Do not publish it as fact. Absence of confirmation is not confirmation of absence, in either direction.
  • Publish the uncertainty if the uncertainty is the story. "This clip is circulating widely, we have not been able to verify it, and here is what we checked" is a legitimate and increasingly valuable piece of journalism.
  • Never accuse. "We could not verify this" and "this is fake" are entirely different claims, and only one of them needs evidence you do not have.
  • Say what you did. Showing the method is what moves trust, and trust is already under four in ten.

Climb the ladder, then break it ★ 14 min · everyone runs it

The desk starts tonight with reverse search already on - 2 out of 10, because you did that much in b1. Add the rest one at a time and read the failure text on the items still red, not just the number. Then, at 10 out of 10, switch on the sixth toggle and look at the second counter before you look at the first.

At 2/10: reverse search alone. Read what is still failing - it is mostly documents and domain judgment, not pictures.

+ Metadata and C2PA: 4/10. The reader-submitted synthetic image and the edited rally photo both flip. Three minutes each.

+ Two sources: 6/10. The resignation and the bypass claim flip. This is the expensive rung, and it is where matched errors live.

+ Primary document: 8/10. Both document items flip. Note that these are the ones that produce the corrections readers remember.

+ Expert call: 10/10. Five minutes each on the spliced audio and the satellite image. The cheapest rung on the board finished the job.

Now switch on ⚠ AI detector: 9/10 and 1 false accusation. Read the top row - the genuine wire photo of the council vote.

Do this with your own near-miss now Open the thing you nearly ran and route it: which check applied, did you run it, and how many minutes would it have cost? Almost everyone finds the answer was 90 seconds.

The detector conversation, step by step ★ 8 min · explore

23:40, and the department has bought a detector. Step through what happens with the toggle ON, then turn it off and run it again with Metadata + C2PA on instead. Same night, same images, two completely different newsrooms.

Run it with the detector ON. Read the flags step carefully: one of the two was already caught by file history.

Read the step explaining what the tool actually outputs, and what your own pipeline did to the image before it arrived.

Read the meeting step. Somebody's contract supplier is now quietly treated as a faker on a number nobody in the room can explain.

Turn the detector OFF and Metadata ON. Same question, answerable, and no honest contributor gets accused.

Say the rule out loud: we publish what we can source, not what a score says. Absence of provenance means we hold - it never means we accuse.

Real world

The provenance base rate is the reason this keeps happening. Fewer than 1% of news images and videos published globally carry C2PA metadata, with pilot adoption growing at agencies and broadcasters including the BBC. Because almost nothing is signed, newsrooms reach for a tool that promises to work without signatures - and that tool is guessing. The way out is slow and collective: sign your own output, ask suppliers for theirs, and treat absence as normal rather than as suspicion. b7 covers signing what you publish.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 40 min total

  • Run the full ladder until you can predict which items flip at each rung. Then run the detector toggle once and write one sentence on what it contributed.
  • Take the last ten things your desk published. For each, name which check applied and whether it was run. Count honestly.
  • Time yourself on a real reverse image search. If it is over two minutes, fix the workflow - the standard is not the problem.
  • Pick one story where you would have needed an expert and find the person now, before you need them. Five minutes is only cheap if you already have the number.
  • Write the routing rules on one card and put it on the desk. Six lines, not a policy document.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Which check is the best value on the list, and which one is nearly always skipped?

Five minutes with someone who knows the domain caught both the spliced audio and the misdated satellite image on the desk. It is the cheapest rung on the board and the one that requires the most humility, which is why it goes first when the clock is running.

2 · A press release says the new terminal cuts emissions 40%. Which check applies first?

The most common way a newsroom publishes something false is not fabrication, it is a genuine figure inside a sentence that the filing does not support. Per what, measured how, by when, compared to what - and only the primary document answers those.

3 · All five checks come back inconclusive on a widely circulating clip. What do you do?

"We could not verify this" and "this is fake" are different claims, and only one of them needs evidence you do not have. Showing the method is itself valuable journalism, and it is what actually moves trust rather than asserting it.
