Leading the change

Five sessions of analysis end here, in a room where people are worried about their jobs and are right to be. Upskilling and AI infrastructure is one of the five themes the Reuters Institute's 2026 forecasts name for the year, which in practice means a small number of roles that did not exist two years ago, created almost entirely by moving people rather than hiring them. Tonight we build those roles out of a 40-person newsroom, look at the two ways a tool gets introduced and what each costs, run the one-hour training that actually lands, and write a 90-day plan with a kill criterion on every line. The test of the plan is simple: it has to be the same plan whether you are saying it to the newsroom or to the board.

Part 1 · the org chart moves before anything else does

Roles that did not exist two years ago 8 min live

Three functions have quietly become necessary in newsrooms of every size: a verification desk as a named function rather than a thing everyone does badly when rushed, an archive and data role that makes decades of material findable and usable, and an owner for provenance and signing. The honest part, which nobody puts on the slide, is that these are created by redeploying people. Almost no publisher in the small or medium band is hiring three new heads in the year described in session a5.

LiveThe three roles, and what each one actually does5 min▶
RoleWhat it ownsWhere the people come from
Verification deskThe gate. Runs the five checks on anything contested, holds the standard when the clock is running, trains everyone else, and is the escalation point at 22:00 rather than a group chat.Three experienced reporters who already do this instinctively and currently get no credit for it.
Archive and dataMakes decades of tape, text and photography findable, and owns the datasets behind data-led stories. The port expansion filings live here, not in someone's downloads folder.One from production, one from digital, and the person who already knows where everything is.
Provenance and signingSigning what you publish, checking credentials on what arrives, and owning the corrections trail. Usually a part of the archive role rather than a whole post.Carved from the same three, with a named individual accountable rather than a team collectively responsible.

Two design rules for all three. One name, not a committee - a function that "everyone owns" is a function nobody staffs on a bad night. And seniority, not availability - the verification desk cannot be the most junior people, because its entire job is to say no to a story someone senior wants to publish.

Real world

The Harbour Ledger's gate was already staffed - just not on paper. Three reporters were doing the checking for the whole newsroom out of instinct and goodwill, with no time allocated, no authority to hold a story, and no mention in anyone's objectives. Naming the desk did not create the work. It made the work visible, fundable and defensible, and it stopped two of those three people leaving.

LiveRedeployment is a promise, and promises get audited3 min▶

Moving six people into new functions is a real intervention, and the room will read it as a signal about what comes next. Reporting capacity genuinely drops - nineteen reporters is not twenty-two - so say plainly what you will stop covering, or the room will assume the same output is expected from fewer people, which is how you get worse checking rather than better. A redeployment that is really a cut will be recognised within a month: create the verification desk, then quietly fail to backfill the next leaver, and you have spent the trust to buy a spreadsheet saving.

New roles also need new objectives. A verification lead measured on story output will publish rather than hold. Whatever you measure them on is the job you actually created - and a plan with no stated reversal condition is a plan nobody in the room believes.

Part 2 · the part that is not about technology

Change without spending the trust 7 min live

The newsroom's fear is not the technology. Most of your reporters have used these tools and formed a view. The fear is that the technology is the argument for cutting them - and often, in this industry, it has been exactly that. Which means every rollout carries a second message underneath the first one, and the room hears the second message first. Here are the two paths a tool takes into a newsroom, and what each costs.

LiveWhat to tell the newsroom, and what to tell the board5 min▶

These must not be two different stories, and the reason is practical rather than moral: both audiences talk to journalists, and one of them is journalists. The framing legitimately differs. The content cannot.

QuestionTo the newsroomTo the board
What is being automated?Named tasks: transcripts, rough cuts, subtitles, first-pass archive search, monitoring. Preparation, not assertion.The same list, with the hours it returns and where those hours go.
What is not?The gate, the claim, the attribution, the correction, and any byline.The same, framed as the liability position it actually is.
Are jobs going?The honest answer, including "I do not yet know" if that is true, plus the date by which you will know.The same answer and the same date.
What if the economics do not improve?The plan you would follow, said before you need it.The identical plan, with the numbers from session a5 attached.

The single most damaging sentence in this whole change is "efficiency" said to the board and "augmentation" said to the newsroom in the same week. Everyone finds out, and the finding out costs more than the truth ever would have.

LiveWhy Path B works, mechanically3 min▶

It is not that Path B is gentler. It produces evidence the room can check, and it puts the proof in the hands of colleagues rather than management. The problem is named by the people who have it, so it is a problem worth fixing. The demonstration is done by a peer on real stories, which carries more weight than any vendor session or all-staff email. Failure is cheap: one desk wasted a month, where killing a mandated tool means somebody senior admitting a public error, so bad tools survive far longer than they deserve to. And it generates the honest measurement - what changed on the work, rather than how many people logged in once.

Part 3 · the practical layer

Training, measurement, and the first 90 days cards · read in any order

Four practical problems every editor hits in the first quarter: what to teach, how to know whether it worked, what to do first, and what to do when your reporters are already further ahead than you are.

LiveThe one-hour training that actually lands4 min▶

Most newsroom AI training fails because it teaches tools, and tools change every quarter. Teach two things, both of which outlive any product:

  • The five checks (30 minutes). Reverse search, metadata and C2PA, two genuinely independent sources, the primary document, the expert call. Run one real inbound item from your own week through all five, live, in front of the room. Include the detector, and show why it is not a check.
  • The policy tiers (20 minutes). What may be drafted with AI assistance, what needs a named human check before it leaves the building, and what may never be generated at all. From session a4. Three tiers, examples for each, no ambiguity in the middle.
  • Questions (10 minutes). Take the hostile ones first and answer them straight, starting with the job one.

That is the whole hour. No tool demo, no vendor, no slide about the history of neural networks. When somebody asks which product to use, the honest answer is that the checks and the tiers are the same whichever product you buy, and that is precisely why they are what you teach.

Repeat it, do not extend it Run the same hour again in three months with a different real item rather than building an advanced module. Repetition is what turns the five checks into a reflex at 22:00, and the reflex is the entire objective.
LiveMeasuring whether any of this worked4 min▶

Tool usage is the easiest thing to measure and it tells you almost nothing. Seats, logins and prompt counts describe activity, not outcomes, and they can all rise while your journalism gets worse.

Do not measureMeasureWhy it is the real signal
Licence seats usedCorrections traced to a missing checkGoes down when the gate is actually working, and names the failure precisely
Prompts per journalistTime from tip to verifiedThe thing the desk was created to improve, measured on real stories
Training completionShare of contested items that got all five checksTells you whether the standard holds when the clock is running
Hours "saved"Stories the archive role unlocked, and where returned hours wentValue created, not activity performed - and hours that vanish into workload are not a benefit the room will believe
Tools evaluatedWhether the desk running Path B kept using it after month oneThe only adoption number that means anything is voluntary continued use

That last row is the one to hold on to. If a tool returns two hours a night and those hours quietly become more output rather than more checking, you have automated preparation and spent the gain on volume - which is how a newsroom ends up faster, busier and less accurate at the same time.

LiveThe first 90 days4 min▶

Three months, three commitments, and nothing else. The temptation is to run six workstreams because six looks like leadership. Six is how none of them finish.

Days 1-30: name the owner and publish the policy. The verification desk gets a named lead with real seniority and protected time, the a4 policy tiers get written down and circulated, and nothing is bought. Days 31-60: one desk, one real problem. Path B - one team, one named problem they chose, a month of use on real stories, and a written note at the end saying what changed and what did not. Days 61-90: train the room and set the baseline. Run the one-hour training, take the a5 numbers to the board with the gap visible, and record today's value for the four outcome measures so that in six months you can tell whether anything moved.

Every line gets a kill criterion, written before you start, in the form "if X has not happened by date Y, we stop and say so." A plan without kill criteria is a plan that only ever grows.

LiveWhen the newsroom is further ahead than the leadership3 min▶

This is now the common case, and it is treated as a threat when it is the best asset in the building. Your reporters are already using these tools, mostly on personal accounts, mostly sensibly, and mostly without telling you.

  • Do not lead with a ban. A ban you cannot enforce moves the same activity somewhere you cannot see, and the first thing you lose is the ability to know what happened when something goes wrong.
  • Ask what they are already doing, without consequences attached. Say clearly that nobody is in trouble. You will learn more in twenty minutes than from any vendor evaluation, you will discover which desk should run Path B, and whoever has quietly built the best workflow should be teaching the one-hour session rather than hiding it.
  • Your job is the boundary, not the expertise. You do not need to be the most fluent person in the building. You need to own the gate, the tiers and the corrections trail, and you need to be honest about which of those is not currently working.
Self-studyAutomation and agents, read honestly4 min read▶

Automation and agents reshaping newsrooms is one of the five recurring themes in the Reuters Institute's 2026 expert forecasts, alongside audiences reaching news through AI, rising demand for verification, upskilling and AI infrastructure, and AI empowering data journalism. Read as a leadership problem rather than a product announcement, it means this:

  • These tools arrive whether or not they help you. They will appear in your CMS, your analytics, your transcription service and your ad stack as features you did not choose and cannot decline separately. Plan for arrival, not adoption.
  • The gate is the constant. Whatever an agent does upstream, something still has to assert in public that a claim is true. Every theme on that list makes the gate more load-bearing, not less - which is the reason the verification desk in Part 1 is the first commitment rather than the third.
  • Beware the efficiency loop. Agents produce more material, more material needs more checking, and the checking is the thing that was cut to pay for the agents. Notice that loop before you are inside it.

The practical, desk-level version of all of this is the producer track. Session b1 sets the frame, b2 is the gate in full, and b10 puts one story through every format with its provenance intact.

Build-along · everyone writes one

Write your 90-day plan, with kill criteria ★ 18 min · one page only

One page. Three initiatives, no more. Every line carries a named owner, a date, and the condition under which you stop. If you cannot write the kill criterion, you have not defined the initiative well enough to start it - and that discovery is worth the session on its own.

Write the verification owner's name. An actual person, not a team. If you cannot name them tonight, write the date by which you will, and treat that date as the plan's first deadline.

List the three initiatives for the next 90 days. Cross out everything beyond three. The fourth one is the reason none of them will land.

For each, write the kill criterion in one sentence: "if X has not happened by date Y, we stop and say so." Be specific enough that a colleague could check it without you.

Write the four outcome measures and today's value for each - corrections traced to a missing check, time from tip to verified, share of contested items fully checked, stories the archive role unlocked - then pick which desk runs Path B and what problem they already say they have. Do not choose the problem for them, and do not choose the tool yet.

Write, in full sentences, what you will say to the newsroom. Include the job question and answer it honestly, including "I do not yet know, and here is when I will."

Now read it as if you were presenting it to the board. Every place you would change a word, stop and work out which of the two versions is the true one. Keep that one and use it in both rooms.

The test for the whole plan Could this page be leaked to your own newsroom tomorrow with no edits and no explanation? If yes, you have a plan. If no, you have two stories, and you have already started spending the only thing you cannot buy back.
Homework

Try it yourself - this month ◐ one quarter, three commitments

  • Name the verification owner and tell them, in person, with protected time attached and an objective that rewards holding a story rather than shipping it.
  • Run the one-hour training: the five checks on a real item from your own week, the three policy tiers, ten minutes of hostile questions taken first. Book the repeat for three months out on the same day.
  • Write the newsroom version and the board version of your plan, put them side by side, and keep only the version that is true in both rooms. Then ask three reporters what AI tools they already use, with no consequences attached. Whoever has the best workflow teaches the next session. Record today's value for the four outcome measures, and put a kill criterion on every initiative already running, including the ones you inherited.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Where does the verification desk in a 40-person newsroom actually come from?

Almost nobody in the small or medium band is hiring for this in the year session a5 described. Option C is what most newsrooms already have, and a function everyone owns is a function nobody staffs on a bad night. Redeployment is the honest answer, and the honesty includes naming what you will stop covering with nineteen reporters instead of twenty-two.

2 · Why does the "one desk solves one real problem" path build adoption where a mandate does not?

A mandate assumes the problem and puts management's authority behind the tool, which means killing a bad tool requires someone senior to admit a public error - so bad tools survive. Path B produces evidence colleagues can check and generates the honest measurement: what changed on the work, not how many people logged in once.

3 · What is the test for the plan you write in this session?

"Efficiency" upstairs and "augmentation" downstairs in the same week is the single most damaging move available to you, because both audiences talk to journalists and one of them is journalists. Six workstreams is how none of them finish, and adoption metrics rise happily while the journalism gets worse.
