Distribution when search stops sending

The port story is finished. It has a verified inbound trail from b2, a number decomposed against the filing in b3, a headline that survives the correction column from b4, an audio cut, a video cut, signed images and a 1998 council promise pulled out of the archive. None of that matters if nobody reads it. For twenty years the answer to "how will people find this" was search, and search is being withdrawn: media leaders expect referrals down about 43% within three years, a fifth of them expect losses above 75%, and Google traffic to publishers already fell by roughly a third in a single year. Tonight is the practitioner's half of that problem. Not the business model - the reporter's question: how does this specific story reach a person now.

Part 0

Where this sits in the chain

Distribution is stage 5 of the publish chain, and it is the only stage where the work is already done and can still be wasted. Everything before tonight was about whether the story is true and whether it is well made. Tonight is about whether it arrives. The honest framing is that the channel which used to do most of the arriving is going away, the replacements are smaller, and no combination of them adds back to what search used to deliver. That is not a reason to despair, it is a reason to stop optimising for a machine that no longer forwards traffic and start building the two or three routes that answer to you.

Part 1 · the withdrawal, from the desk

What is actually leaving 7 min live

Four numbers set the floor, and they are the whole reason this session exists. Media leaders surveyed by the Reuters Institute expect search referrals to fall about 43% within three years, and a fifth of them expect losses above 75%. Google traffic to publishers has already fallen by roughly a third in a year. The damage is not evenly spread: small publishers running 1,000 to 10,000 daily page views lost about 60% of their search referrals against about 22% for the largest. If you work at a 40-person outlet like the Harbour Ledger, the size effect is the number that concerns you, not the average.

LiveWhat this means for a reporter, not a publisher4 min▶

Most coverage of this treats it as a revenue story for executives. At the desk it is a much simpler and more personal change:

  • The story that used to find its own audience now has to be handed over. A well-optimised piece on a searchable topic used to accumulate readers for months without you doing anything. That passive accumulation is the part that is going.
  • Your remaining audience is people who already chose you. Direct visits, the newsletter list, the podcast subscribers, and the person who forwards your link to a group chat. Every one of those is a relationship rather than a ranking.
  • Small newsrooms are hit hardest, and that is measured. About 60% against about 22% is not a rounding difference. If you are small, plan for the small-publisher number.
Real world

The Harbour Ledger's port story is exactly the kind that used to live on search. "Port expansion emissions" is a searchable phrase, the story is evergreen for months, and in the old world it would have collected readers slowly all through the consultation period. In the new one, if the newsroom does not hand it to people directly on the day, the consultation closes and the story was read by the people who were already reading.

Self-studyWhat not to conclude from these numbers3 min read▶
  • "So publish more." Volume worked when search rewarded coverage of everything. It is the worst possible response to a world where commodity coverage is the exact thing an answer engine now handles without you.
  • "So chase the platform of the month." Every borrowed channel eventually does to you what search just did. Building your distribution on a surface you do not control is how you get here twice.
  • "So it is over." Direct relationships, original material and a reason to be trusted are all things you can still build, and nobody can withdraw them by changing a ranking algorithm.
Part 2 · honesty about what you control

Being cited when the answer is written by a machine 7 min live

There is a large and growing industry selling newsrooms a way to rank inside AI answers. Treat it the way you treat an AI detector: a confident product answering a question nobody can actually verify. Nobody outside those systems knows what makes them cite one source over another, and the people who claim they do are selling you the old SEO fantasy with a new noun. So here is the honest version. Some things plausibly help, because they make you the thing there is a reason to point at. One thing you cannot control at all, and no amount of spending changes that. Aim at the first list.

LiveThe plausible list, and the thing you cannot control4 min▶

Plausibly helps, and is good journalism anyway:

  • Be the primary source, not the aggregation. If your piece is a summary of three other pieces, the honest citation is the three other pieces.
  • Structure and dates that are unambiguous. When the story happened, when it was updated, what changed. A machine reading your page should not have to guess the year.
  • Attribution that names people and documents. "The council said" is weaker than "the harbour authority's 2031 projection, filed 14 March, states".
  • Material nobody else has. Your archive, your dataset, your interview. This is the only item on the list that competitors cannot simply also do.

You cannot control: whether any given system cites you at all, how often, or with what wording. That is not pessimism, it is the accurate description. Anyone who tells you otherwise is describing a crawler they have not seen.

Self-studyWhy "optimise for the crawler" is the wrong instinct3 min read▶

The SEO era taught a habit that transfers badly. The habit was: find what the ranking system rewards, produce that shape, collect the traffic. It worked because there was a stable, observable, gameable ranking and a click at the end of it.

  • The reward function is not observable. You cannot A/B test your way into a system whose citation behaviour you cannot measure, and which changes without notice.
  • The unfakeable part is the material. A named source and a document you obtained cannot be generated by a competitor with a bigger content budget. Everything else can.

So the instruction is "be the thing worth citing", and it is deliberately unsexy. It happens to be identical to the instruction the trade already had.

Part 3 · the routes that answer to you

The channels, in order of how much you own them 6 min live

Rank your channels by one question: if the owner of that surface changed their mind tomorrow, what happens to you. That ranking puts the newsletter first, direct visits second, your own apps and feeds third, and every platform a distant fourth. It is not a moral ranking, it is a risk one.

LiveThe newsletter is the only channel you fully own3 min▶

The Harbour Ledger's daily newsletter is a list of email addresses of people who asked to hear from you. No intermediary ranks it, throttles it or deprecates it. That single property makes it worth more than a far larger platform following.

  • Export the list and know where the export lives. A list you cannot take with you is not a list you own, it is a list your provider owns.
  • The lead-in is journalism, not a teaser. Write the actual finding in the email. A reader who learns something from the email and does not click is still a reader you kept.
LiveSocial cuts that do not misrepresent the story3 min▶

b6 made the point on video and it applies to every clip you post: a cut changes context, and a cut posted without context is a claim you have made. The distribution version of that rule:

  • The clip must be true standing alone. Assume nobody clicks through. If the 20 seconds you posted would need the article to not be misleading, it is the wrong 20 seconds.
  • Never trim a qualification out. "40% emissions cut" is a misrepresentation of the Ledger's own story. "40% per container, on a projection, by 2031" is the story.
LiveWriting the top for a reader who arrived with no context3 min▶

A reader who followed a link out of an AI answer has been told something already, possibly something wrong, and knows nothing about your coverage. The old assumption that a reader arrived from your home page, or from a search phrase that told you what they wanted, is gone.

  • Say what this is in the first sentence. Which port, which council, which claim, which date. No "as we reported yesterday" before the reader knows what yesterday was.
  • Restate the claim before you assess it. If your first line assumes the reader knows the 40% figure exists, half of them are lost by line two.
LiveMeasuring what is left3 min▶

Pageviews were a good metric when traffic was abundant and cheap. In a world where the cheap traffic is being withdrawn, a pageview count mostly measures how much of the withdrawal has happened yet. Better instruments:

Self-studyWhat small newsrooms specifically should stop doing4 min read▶

Small publishers lost about 60% of their search referrals against about 22% for the largest. The strategic implication is uncomfortable and specific: the traffic a 40-person newsroom was chasing is the traffic that has left, and chasing it harder is spending scarce reporter hours on a market that closed.

  • Stop producing commodity coverage. The results round-up, the weather explainer, the rewrite of the national wire story. An answer engine now serves those itself, without you, and it always will.
  • Stop the SEO-shaped explainer. "What time is the council meeting" was a traffic product, not journalism, and it is the first thing to be absorbed.
  • Start with the thing only you can do. The Ledger is the only newsroom on earth with the berth-times spreadsheet and the 1998 council promise. That is the whole competitive position, and it is enough.
Build-along · everyone runs it

Distribute the port story ★ 18 min · four channels, one story

The story is published. You now have four surfaces to fill and one absolute constraint: every channel version is derived from the published piece and contains no fact that is not in it. The moment a social caption asserts something the story does not, you have published an unverified claim under the masthead without anyone editing it.

Open the published story and the correction column from b4. Everything tonight derives from those two, and nothing derives from your memory of the reporting.

Site. Rewrite the top for a reader with no context: which port, which claim, which date, and the finding that the 40% is per container, on a projection, by 2031.

Newsletter. Write the actual finding into the email, not a teaser. Name the document. End with a real reply-to address and ask the list what they know.

Audio and social. Take the 40-second tease from the b5 cut and one clip from the b6 council-chamber cut, attribution burnt in. Check each is true standing alone.

Run the fact-diff: list every claim, number and name in the four versions and tick each one against the published story. Anything with no tick gets cut, not sourced later.

Why the second prompt is the dangerous one It asks for three things at once that a newsroom cannot allow. "Maximum reach" has no truth constraint. "Trending keywords" invites terms the story does not support. "Make the numbers sound dramatic" is a direct instruction to strip the qualification off the 40% figure, which is the exact error the Ledger spent eight sessions not making. It also optimises for a channel that is being withdrawn.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 40-50 min total

  • Pull your own traffic by source for the last 12 months. Not one month - twelve, so you can see a trend rather than a bad week. Write down the search share at the start and at the end.
  • Take one story you published this month and rewrite its opening for a reader who arrived with no context at all. Time how long it takes. It is usually under ten minutes.
  • Name the one channel you would defend if you could keep only one, and say what would happen to your work next week if you lost it tomorrow.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your newsroom runs about 6,000 daily page views. Which figure should you plan against?

The loss is measured as uneven by size: about 60% for small publishers, about 47% for medium and about 22% for large. Planning a 6,000-view newsroom against the large-publisher number is planning against someone else's problem.

2 · A vendor offers to guarantee your stories are cited by AI answer engines. What is the honest response?

Whether a given system cites you at all is outside your control, and the reward function is not observable or testable. The list of things that plausibly help is identical to good journalism, which is why it is worth doing either way.

3 · Why must a social cut of the port story be true standing alone?

A caption reading "40% emissions cut" misrepresents the Ledger's own story to more people than read the story. The qualification - per container, on a projection, by 2031 - has to survive every trim, and the caption goes through the desk like a headline.
