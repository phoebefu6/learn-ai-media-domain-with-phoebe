Learn AI Media with Phoebe · Editor track · Session 5 of 6

Audience economics after search

This is the hardest session in the editor track and the one that decides the other five. News executives surveyed by the Reuters Institute expect search referrals to fall about 43% within three years, and a fifth expect losses above 75%. Google traffic to publishers already fell by roughly a third in the year to November. The damage is deeply uneven: small publishers lost about 60% of their search referrals, medium about 47%, large about 22% - which means the titles with the least cushion took the biggest hit. Tonight we do not do optimism. We size the hole, size the four things that could fill it, admit that for most publishers they do not add up, and then work out what a 40-person newsroom can actually do about it.

Part 0

Before we start, one ground rule

No session in this track will lie to you about the arithmetic, and this is the session where that costs something. Everywhere else in the industry you will be sold a replacement channel, an SEO fix for AI answers, or a licensing deal that sounds bigger than it is. What follows is what the research actually says, sized honestly, applied to the Harbour Ledger - 40 staff, a site, a daily newsletter, a weekly podcast and a two-person video desk - because that is roughly the size where the numbers get personal. If you brought 12 months of your own traffic data, you will leave with a three-year number you can defend.

Part 1 · size the hole first

The referral withdrawal, in numbers 8 min live

Three numbers, and you need all three. Forecast: news executives expect search referrals to fall about 43% within three years, and a fifth of them expect losses above 75%. Already happened: Google search traffic to publishers fell by about a third globally in the year to November. Distribution: the loss is not spread evenly. Small publishers running 1,000 to 10,000 daily page views lost about 60% of their search referrals, medium publishers about 47%, and large publishers about 22%. The forecast is not the story. The distribution is.

LiveWhy the smallest publishers were hit hardest4 min▶

This is not bad luck and it is not a penalty. It follows directly from what small publishers ranked for:

  • They ranked for commodity queries. Opening hours, bin collection dates, what time the match starts, what the council decided, what the weather will do. These are exactly the questions an answer engine can answer in the result itself, with no click.
  • Nobody was searching for them by name. A large masthead absorbs a search decline because a meaningful share of its audience arrives by typing the brand, opening the app, or clicking a newsletter. That traffic is not a search referral and does not fall when search falls.
  • The commodity traffic paid for the rest. The bin collection page funded the council reporter. When the commodity layer goes, the funding for the reporting layer goes with it, even though the reporting layer is the part that was actually valuable.
  • The cushion is thinnest where the hit is hardest. A large title losing 22% has reserves, a diversified mix and a subscriptions team. A small title losing 60% has three months.
Real world

The Harbour Ledger's shape is the dangerous shape. 40 staff, a site that has always ranked well locally, a newsletter treated as a side product, and a large share of arrivals coming from search on exactly the kind of query an answer engine now handles. It is not small enough to be a hobby and not large enough to have brand pull. Every publisher in that band should be running the numbers in Part 3 this quarter, not next year.

LiveWhat the 43% forecast does and does not tell you3 min▶

It is a forecast from 280 media leaders across 51 countries, and forecasts by interested parties deserve care in both directions:

  • It is a central case, not a floor. A fifth of those leaders expect losses above 75%. If your traffic profile looks like the small-publisher band, plan against that tail rather than the average.
  • It is about referrals, not audience. People still want to know what the council decided. What broke is the route by which they arrived and the ad impression that came with it.
  • The past-year data is not a forecast at all. The roughly one-third fall in Google traffic already happened. Treat the 43% as the direction of travel from a floor that has already dropped.
  • It does not tell you your number. Two publishers with the same headcount can have wildly different referral dependence. That is Part 3, and it is why you brought your data.
Part 2 · four candidates, sized honestly

What can actually replace it 7 min live

Four candidates get proposed in every boardroom: direct audience, licensing, reader revenue, and cutting the newsroom. Each is real. None is the size of the hole. Just over two thirds of surveyed leaders expect AI licensing deals to bring at least some revenue within three years, but most see it as a minor source - and "minor" is the operative word in a sentence people keep quoting without it. Here is the bridge from today's mix to a plausible three-year mix, with the part that does not close left visible rather than smoothed over.

LiveSizing each candidate without flattering it4 min▶
CandidateWhat it really isHonest size
Direct audienceNewsletter, app, and a reading habit strong enough that people arrive without a linkThe only one that compounds, and the slowest to build. Years, not quarters.
LicensingDeals with AI companies for content and archive accessJust over two thirds expect some revenue in three years. Most expect it to be minor. Lumpy and not renewable by right.
Reader revenueSubscriptions, memberships, donationsReal and durable, but it grows from the audience you already hold - and referral loss is what shrinks that intake.
Cutting the newsroomFewer people making lessCloses the arithmetic immediately and shrinks the only asset that generates the other three. It is a decision, not a strategy.

Notice the trap in row two. Licensing is the only candidate that arrives as a cheque without needing you to build anything, which is exactly why it gets over-weighted in board conversations. "Two thirds expect some revenue" is quoted constantly. "Most expect it to be minor" is quoted almost never.

LiveThe sentence to say when they do not add up3 min▶

For most publishers in the small and medium bands, the four candidates do not sum to the hole. Pretending otherwise is how newsrooms end up doing three half-funded transformations at once and then cutting anyway, two years later, with the trust already spent.

  • Say the gap out loud, with a number. "Under the central case we are short by X, and under the tail case by Y" is a sentence a board can work with. "We are exploring new revenue streams" is not.
  • Then say what you will protect. A gap you have named lets you choose what survives it. A gap you have not named gets closed by whatever is easiest to cut, which is usually reporting.
  • Do not let a pilot stand in for a plan. A licensing conversation, a paywall test and a newsletter refresh are three experiments. They are not a bridge until you have sized them.
  • Separate the two decisions. How you grow revenue and how much newsroom you can carry are different questions on different clocks. Answering them as one question always resolves in favour of the cut.
The rule of this session Never present a three-year plan that closes to zero on the first slide. If your bridge closes perfectly, you have hidden the assumption that is going to break, and everyone in the room knows it.
Part 3 · after the referral

What you measure and what you defend cards · read in any order

When an answer engine sits between you and your reader, half your dashboard stops describing anything real. This part is about which numbers survive that, what you can and cannot influence about being cited in AI answers, and the one channel that is genuinely yours.

LiveThe metrics that stop meaning anything, and what replaces them4 min▶

Pageviews and sessions were always proxies. They measured arrivals, and arrivals were mostly a function of how well you ranked. With an answer engine in front of you, a falling pageview count can mean you lost readers, or that the same readers got their answer without arriving, or that you stopped ranking for a query you never wanted. Three very different situations, one number.

Stops meaning muchBecauseUse instead
PageviewsCounts arrivals from a route that is being withdrawnReturning readers - people who came back this month having come last month
SessionsOne person, many devices, many accidental entriesNewsletter open habit - the share of subscribers opening 4 or more of the last 8
Search referral volumeFalling for reasons unrelated to your journalismDirect share of traffic - the fraction arriving without an intermediary
Average time on pageDominated by drive-by traffic you are losing anywayDepth per returning reader - what your actual audience reads

The right-hand column has a property the left-hand column never had: it goes up when your journalism gets better and it does not move when a platform changes its mind. That is the whole reason to switch.

LiveBeing cited by AI answers - what you can influence, honestly4 min▶

You will be sold "AI SEO" this year. Here is the honest split, and nobody should be charging you for the first column.

  • You can influence: whether your reporting is the primary source of a fact rather than a restatement of someone else's; whether your pages are structured and readable by machines; whether your name is attached to claims that get repeated; whether you have signed provenance on what you publish.
  • You cannot control: whether a given model cites you, how it phrases your reporting, whether a citation is a link, whether a link is clicked, or whether any of it survives the next model update.
  • The uncomfortable middle: being cited well and getting no traffic is a completely coherent outcome. Citation is reputation, not revenue, and treating it as revenue is how publishers rebuild the exact dependency that just broke.
  • The one durable move: be the origin. An answer engine has to point somewhere for a fact that exists in one place. Original reporting, primary documents and named sources are the only things that cannot be summarised out of existence, because the summary needs them.

The practitioner-level treatment - structured data, how AI referrals actually behave, what to measure - is b9, Distribution and AI answers. Send whoever owns your distribution to it. Do not send them on an SEO course.

LiveThe newsletter is the only channel you own outright3 min▶

Every other channel is rented. Search is rented from an index that just changed its terms. Social is rented from a feed that deprioritised links years ago. AI answers are rented from a model you cannot see. An email list is a set of addresses that belong to you, that arrive without an intermediary deciding, and that you can take with you if you change every other piece of technology you own.

  • Treat it as the product, not the promotion. Most newsrooms staff the newsletter as an afterthought and then wonder why open rates decay. At the Harbour Ledger it is the single highest-leverage asset in the building and it is currently written in the last 40 minutes of the day.
  • Measure habit, not size. A list of 8,000 people who open four times a week is worth more than 40,000 who opened once in March.
  • It survives every scenario in this session. The 43% case and the 75% case both leave the list intact. That is a rare property and it should change your staffing.
  • It is also the fastest thing to ruin. Automated summaries, generic subject lines and daily volume without a voice will empty it faster than any algorithm change.
Real world

The port expansion story is a newsletter story. The press release claiming a 40% emissions cut went to every outlet in the region on the same morning. The version that matters is the one that says what the underlying filing actually claims, sent directly to 8,000 people who already trust the byline, without waiting for anyone's ranking system to agree.

Self-studyWhat this does to local news specifically4 min read▶

The size effect in Part 1 is not only a business fact. Read it again with the civic layer attached:

  • The publishers losing 60% are the ones covering councils. Small local titles are disproportionately the outlets that send a person to the planning meeting, read the filing, and notice that the press release says 40% and the document says something considerably more careful.
  • Nobody replaces that layer. A national title does not cover a harbour authority. An answer engine can summarise the coverage that exists; it cannot generate the coverage that was never made because the reporter's post was cut.
  • The traffic that funded it was the traffic most easily absorbed. Bin collections and opening hours were cross-subsidising democratic accountability, which is a fragile arrangement that mostly worked, and it is the arrangement that just ended.
  • It compounds with the trust floor. Fewer than four in ten people say they trust the news they see. Local titles have historically scored better than national ones. Losing them removes the part of the ecosystem people still believed.

This matters for your session tomorrow because it changes what you are defending. If you run a small title, the argument for keeping the council reporter is not only that readers like it. It is that nobody else in the ecosystem is going to do it, and once the post is gone the capability does not come back.

Build-along · everyone runs it

Model your own three-year number ★ 20 min · bring your data

Open your analytics and a spreadsheet. This takes 20 minutes and produces the only slide from this session that anyone will remember. Do it in the room, out loud, with the actual numbers rather than a placeholder, because the placeholder version is always more comfortable than the real one.

Pull 12 months of traffic by source. You need four buckets only: search referral, direct, newsletter, everything else. Write down today's share for each.

Write down what share of revenue depends on the search-referral bucket. Not a feeling - the actual ad revenue attributable to those arrivals. If you cannot compute it, that is finding number one.

Apply the central scenario: search referral down 43%. Compute the revenue that removes. Write the number in currency, not percentages.

Apply the tail scenario: down 75%. A fifth of surveyed leaders expect this. Compute it too, and put the two numbers side by side on one line.

Divide each gap by your fully loaded cost per head. This is the uncomfortable step and it is the point of the exercise. Say the two headcount numbers out loud in the room.

Now subtract the honest replacement: whatever direct audience, licensing and reader revenue realistically add in three years. Label whatever is left "the gap" and leave it visible.

Finally, name the one channel you would defend if you could only keep one, and say what you would cut to fund defending it. That sentence is your strategy.

If you cannot attribute revenue to referral traffic Do the exercise with page-view share as a rough proxy and mark it clearly as a proxy. Then make fixing the attribution a named piece of work with an owner and a date, because every decision in the next two years depends on that number and right now you are guessing.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 60-90 min total

  • Pull your direct-traffic share for 12 months and plot it as a line. The trend matters more than the level, and most publishers have never looked at it this way.
  • Run both scenarios on your own numbers, 43% and 75%, all the way through to headcount. Put both on one page with the gap left visible.
  • Name the one channel you would defend if you could only keep one, write the reason in a sentence, and check whether your current staffing reflects that answer. It usually does not.
  • Find out which queries your site actually ranked for last year, and sort them by how easily an answer engine handles them. That sorted list is your exposure.
  • Ask your commercial lead for the revenue attributable to search-referral arrivals. If nobody can produce it in a week, that is the first project.
  • Read your last board paper on digital strategy and count how many times licensing is described without the word "minor" attached.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Which publishers lost the most search referrals, and why does it compound?

Small lost about 60%, medium about 47%, large about 22%. The largest titles keep brand search and direct habit, so their decline is absorbable. The smallest were funding their reporting with exactly the commodity traffic that disappeared, which is why the same percentage means something completely different at each size.

2 · Your board says AI licensing will replace the referral revenue. What does the research actually support?

The "two thirds" figure gets quoted constantly and the word "minor" gets dropped almost every time. Licensing is the only candidate that arrives as a cheque without you building anything, which is exactly why it gets over-weighted in the room. Size it honestly and it is one small block on the bridge, not the bridge.

3 · Pageviews are falling. Why is that number now close to uninterpretable, and what replaces it?

Three very different situations produce the same falling line, so the number can no longer tell you which one you are in. The replacements share one property pageviews never had: they rise when your journalism gets better and they do not move when a platform changes its mind.
