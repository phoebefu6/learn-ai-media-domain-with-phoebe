Images and synthetic media

This is the hardest red line in the course, and it is also the shortest. A generated image may illustrate an abstract idea when it is clearly labelled. It may never depict a real event, a real place at a real moment, or a real person doing anything. Everything else on this page - where the label goes, what survives a repost, how you sign what you publish - exists to make that one distinction hold under deadline. Tonight you route four real images through a decision gate, label the one that needs it, sign all of them, and deal with the reader-submitted synthetic image the b1 desk caught.

Part 0

Where this sits in the chain

The Harbour Ledger has a two-person video desk who are also the photographers, which means on most days there is no photographer where the story is. That gap is where generated imagery gets reached for, and it is why this session is a red line rather than a technique. Images sit at stage 4 of the publish chain - Package - but an image is also a claim, which puts it back under stage 2, the gate. If a picture asserts that something looked a certain way, it has to clear the same bar as a sentence that says so.

Part 1 · the red line

Illustration versus depiction 8 min live

The whole rule fits in one distinction, and every newsroom that has embarrassed itself with a generated image got the distinction fuzzy first. A generated image may illustrate an abstract idea - a concept image above a piece on data privacy, a stylised graphic about how a levy works - when it is clearly labelled as generated. It may never depict a real event, a real place at a real moment, or a real person doing anything. Not as a placeholder, not as a "representative" image, not with a caption explaining it, and not internally where somebody might grab it later.

LiveThe failure that follows a fuzzy rule4 min▶

Nobody sets out to fake a news photograph. The failure arrives sideways, in four steps, and each step is individually reasonable:

  • Step one. Flooding breaks in a district your photographers cannot reach. Somebody generates a "representative" image of a flooded street, purely to have something in the slot.
  • Step two. It goes up with a small caption saying the image is illustrative. Internally, everyone knows what it is.
  • Step three. Somebody screenshots the page for a social post. Captions do not survive screenshots. It is now a picture of a flooded street with your masthead on it.
  • Step four. Within one repost it is evidence that a street flooded. You are the source of a false claim you never made in words, and the correction has to explain a thing readers thought was a photograph.

The rule has to be absolute precisely because step one is so defensible. "Representative", "placeholder", "just for the slot" and "obviously not real" are the same decision wearing different clothes. The gate closes at step one, or it does not close at all.

Real world

The reader-submitted image on the b1 desk was caught by file history, not by looking at it. By the time you can see the problem in the pixels, the generation is a year old. The catches that hold up are the boring ones: where the file came from, what tool touched it, who sent it and when. That is a provenance question, which is why it lives at stage 2 of the publish chain and not in a picture editor's eye.

LiveThe mundane version, which does more damage3 min▶

The dramatic failure is a fake flood. The common failure is duller and costs more over a year: generated stock-style images used as decoration. A generic person at a generic laptop above a piece on remote work. A generic harbour above a piece on the port.

  • It trains readers to discount every image you run. Once a reader has spotted two generated decorative images on your site, they apply that suspicion to the photograph your two-person desk got up at 5am for.
  • It devalues what you are actually selling. Local photography is one of the few assets that cannot be scraped, summarised or synthesised out from under you. Diluting it with filler is a strange thing to do voluntarily.
  • It creeps. Decorative generation normalises the tool in the image workflow, and the distance from decoration to depiction is one tired night.

The alternative is usually better anyway: an archive photograph with an honest date, a chart, a document crop, or clean white space under a strong headline. Sessions b3 and b8 both feed this slot.

Part 2 · disclosure that survives contact with the internet

Labels a reader actually notices, and signing your own output 7 min live

A label in 9px grey under the fold is not disclosure, it is a compliance artefact. Disclosure means a reader who spends two seconds with the image knows what it is, which means the label sits where the eye already is, in plain words, and inside the image - because the normal fate of anything you publish is to be separated from its caption within hours. Then the constructive half: signing what you publish, so the file carries its own history when your page no longer surrounds it.

LiveWhere the label goes and what it says4 min▶

Three placements, and you need all three. They fail in different places, which is the point:

  • In the image itself. A visible strip or corner block reading AI-generated illustration, sized to survive a phone thumbnail. This is the only layer that survives a screenshot, and step 3 above is where most people meet your image.
  • In the caption, first, in plain words. Not "image: AI". Write This illustration was generated with AI. It is not a photograph of the port. Naming what it is not is what stops the misreading.
  • In the file. Content Credentials recording that it was generated and by what, so a downstream system can read it when no human is looking.

Two wording rules that save arguments later. Say generated, not "AI-assisted" - assisted covers a colour correction and a total fabrication, so it communicates nothing. And put the disclosure before the descriptive caption, because readers stop reading captions early.

LiveSigning your own output with Content Credentials4 min▶

Content Credentials attach a signed history to a file: what device or tool made it, what edits were applied, by whom. On your own photography this is cheap, straightforward, and the one contribution you can make unilaterally. The reality check that keeps it honest:

  • Fewer than 1% of news images and videos published globally carry C2PA metadata today, with pilot adoption growing at agencies and broadcasters including the BBC. That is a base rate you help raise, not a standard you can rely on.
  • Absence proves nothing. The b1 rule restated, because it is the one people break first: almost every genuine photograph you handle carries no credentials at all. Treating "no C2PA" as suspicious means disbelieving nearly everything true.
  • Presence is asymmetric and useful. A valid credential chain tells you a great deal in seconds. A missing one sends you back to the other four checks, which is where you were anyway.
  • Sign the generated ones too. An illustration whose credentials say "generated" does more work for you than one that quietly says nothing.

The realistic order for a 40-person newsroom: staff photography first, because you control that pipeline end to end. Then labelled illustrations, because those are the ones you most want traceable. Wire and agency material last, and largely not yours to change. Many platforms still strip metadata silently on upload - sign anyway, because the credential survives on the copy people download from you and on the copy you licence.

Part 3 · the cases that actually arrive

Five situations, and what you do in each cards

None of these are hypotheticals. Four came through the Harbour Ledger desk this month, and the fifth is on the b1 desk you already ran.

LiveThe reader-submitted synthetic image, and how to decline it well4 min▶

How it was caught. Not by staring at it, and not by a detector. By file history: the submission carried no capture data of any kind, the file had been created by an image tool rather than a camera, and the timestamps did not fit the story the sender told. The metadata check took under two minutes and reverse search confirmed it. That is the whole method, and it is the same method for the next one.

How to decline it and keep the contributor. Reader submissions are one of the few audience assets a local title still has, so do not accuse.

  • Describe, do not diagnose. "We could not confirm the capture details on this file, so we cannot run it" is true, checkable, and does not call anyone a liar.
  • Ask for the original off the phone, unedited, with its capture data. A genuine contributor produces it in a minute, and this one request resolves most cases without confrontation.
  • Tell them what you can use. Give the route back in: what you need next time, and that you want their pictures. People send generated images far more often out of enthusiasm than malice.
  • Never send a detector score. "Our tool scored it 87%" is unanswerable and unexplainable, and it turns a fixable conversation into a grievance.
LiveEnhancement versus manipulation on a real photograph4 min▶

Everything above concerns generated images. This card concerns real ones, where the line is older than AI and has moved only because the tools got better at inventing detail.

OperationWhere it fallsWhy
CropEnhancement, with a limitFine for framing. Not fine when the crop removes the context that changes the meaning: the counter-protest, the empty half of the room.
Global colour and exposureEnhancementDarkroom practice. It changes how a scene reads, not what is in it.
Noise reductionEnhancement, until it inventsClassical denoise removes. AI denoise reconstructs plausible detail. On a face or a number plate, that is fabrication.
Generative fill, object removal, sky replacementManipulationNew pixels asserting things that were not there. Never on a news photograph, in any amount.
UpscalingManipulation in practiceAn upscaler guesses. If the resulting detail could be read as evidence, it is evidence you invented.

The working test: does the operation remove information, or add it? Removing is usually enhancement. Adding is manipulation, even when the addition is invisible and even when it is "just cleaning it up". Log every edit beyond crop and global colour, and put it in the caption when it matters.

LiveThe satellite image, and why it needed an expert rather than a tool3 min▶

An image reached the b1 desk said to show new construction at a disputed site connected to the port expansion. The question was never "is this generated". It was: what sensor, what capture date, what resolution, what is the shadow angle telling you, and is that shape a new structure or a stockpile that has sat there for years.

  • A detector cannot answer any of those. Every one is domain knowledge about how the image was made and what is in the frame.
  • The check that worked was the expert call. Five minutes with someone who reads satellite imagery for a living - the cheapest check on the list, and the first one cut when the clock runs.
  • The primary document mattered as much. The imagery provider's own capture record gave a date, and the date did not match the claim being made about it.
  • What you publish is the reasoning. "An analyst at [institution] reviewed the imagery and dated the capture to X" is publishable. A tool score is not.

This is the general shape: for anything technical, the real risk is not that the image is fake. It is that the image is real and is being described wrongly.

LiveWhen a competitor publishes a synthetic image as real3 min▶

This happens, and it will happen faster than you can match it. The instinct is either to match the story or to write a gleeful piece about them. Both are traps.

  • Do not match it. The competitive pressure to have the same picture is exactly the pressure that produced their error. Being second and right is a position you can defend on the record.
  • Check before you crow. Run your own five checks on their image: reverse search, capture data, the source they credited. A wrong debunk is worse than a wrong story.
  • Tell them first, quietly. One call to their desk before anything is published. It is what you would want, it often ends the problem in ten minutes, and it costs you nothing.
  • If it stays up and it matters, report it flat. Here is the image, here is what our checks found, here is what the source says. No adjectives. Today it is them, and the value of a norm is that it protects you on the day it is your desk.
Self-studyWhy detection tools are still not on the check list4 min read▶

Every session in this track gets asked the same question, and images are where it is asked hardest: surely for this we buy a detector. The answer is still no, for structural reasons rather than quality ones.

  • It answers the wrong question. A detector guesses whether pixels were generated. You need to know where a file came from and what happened to it, which is provenance.
  • Your own pipeline destroys the signal. By the time an image reaches your desk it has been resized, recompressed and colour-corrected several times, and those steps look like generation to a detector.
  • A probability is not publishable. You cannot put a score in a correction, defend it to a supplier, or say it to a contributor you have just accused.
  • The asymmetry is brutal. A false negative costs one story. A false accusation against a real photographer costs a relationship and, over enough of them, the masthead.

The b1 desk result, stated plainly. At 10 out of 10 - every check on, everything caught, including the genuinely synthetic reader submission, caught by file history - switching the detector on dropped the score to 9 and produced 1 false accusation against a real wire photograph of a council vote. Zero additional catches, one accusation against a supplier. A tool that adds no catches and adds accusations has a negative contribution, however reassuring it is to have bought something. Spend the budget instead on an image-desk routine that runs reverse search and metadata on every inbound image in under three minutes, and on the time to make the expert call.

Build-along

Route four images, label one, sign all of them ★ 22 min · everyone runs it

Four images are on the desk for tomorrow's port expansion follow-up: a flooded street from the district your photographers could not reach, a concept image for a sidebar on how emissions per container are calculated, a portrait of the council leader, and a photograph your own desk shot at the berth last Tuesday. Run each through the gate out loud before you touch anything.

Gate 1 on all four. Say for each whether it depicts a real event, place or person. Three of the four do, and those three cannot be generated, full stop - so the flooded street runs as no image, or as a chart, or as an archive photo with an honest date.

Gate 2 on the survivor. Only the emissions-calculation concept image clears Gate 1, because it depicts nothing that exists. Write its label now: an in-image strip, plus a caption whose first sentence says what it is and what it is not.

Caption the real photograph. Your berth photograph needs date, location, photographer and what is in frame. This is the one an archive search will find in 2030, which is session b8's problem tonight and your problem forever.

Sign all of them. Add Content Credentials to the berth photograph and the labelled illustration. Confirm the credential survives your CMS export, then check what your main social platform does to it on upload. Write down both answers.

Screenshot test. Screenshot each published image on a phone, crop out the caption, and look at what is left. If a stranger could mistake the illustration for a photograph, the label failed and you go back to step 2.

✅ Good prompt - writes a caption from verified facts only and refuses to describe what it cannot see 
You are drafting an image caption for a news website. House style: sentence
case, under 30 words, date and location always present.

Below are the ONLY facts you may use. They come from our shot log and have
been confirmed by the photographer.

FACTS:
  photographer: [name]     date and time: [exact]
  location: [exact]        edits applied: [crop / global colour only]
  what is in frame: [only what the photographer confirmed]

Rules you must follow:
1. Use ONLY the facts above. Add no weather, mood, atmosphere, numbers,
   named people, or anything about what the scene "shows" beyond the
   confirmed frame description.
2. If a required element (date, location, photographer) is missing, write
   [UNCONFIRMED] in its place. Do not guess and do not quietly omit it.
3. You cannot see this image. Never describe visual detail that is not in
   the frame description above. If asked to, refuse and say why.
4. If "edits applied" lists anything beyond crop and global colour, append
   a sentence disclosing it.
5. Output exactly two lines: CAPTION, then CREDIT. One version only.
❌ Bad prompt - asks for a news image that depicts a real place 
Generate a photorealistic image of the new port terminal for our story
about the expansion. Make it look like a wide-angle news photograph,
early morning light, some cranes and containers. We can caption it
"illustrative" if anyone asks.
Why the second one fails at the first word, not the last It is not the photorealism that breaks it, and it is not the missing label. The new port terminal is a real place, so the image is depicting, so Gate 1 closes and there is no Gate 2 to reach. The "we can caption it illustrative" clause is the tell: a label being used to license the thing a label cannot license. And the caption dies in the first screenshot anyway, leaving a photorealistic picture of a real terminal, under your masthead, showing a building in a state it has never been in.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 40-60 min total

  • Check your own output. Download three images from your own published site and inspect whether they carry any credentials or capture data at all. Most newsrooms find none, because the CMS strips everything on upload. Find out which step does the stripping - that is the fixable part.
  • Write the one-sentence rule for your desk. The illustration-versus-depiction line in your own words, short enough to say to a freelancer at 11pm, and put where the picture desk actually looks rather than in a policy document.
  • Route your last ten images through the gate. Honestly. Count how many would have failed Gate 1 or Gate 2. If the answer is zero, check whether anyone on the desk has ever been told the rule.
  • Do the screenshot test on any labelled image you already have live, and show the cropped version to someone who does not work with you.
  • Draft the decline email for a submission you cannot confirm, before you need it. Ninety seconds now, and a relationship saved on the night you are angry.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your photographers cannot reach a flooded district tonight. Someone offers a generated "representative" image of a flooded street, clearly captioned as illustrative. What is the answer?

A generated image may illustrate an abstract idea, never depict a real event. And the caption is the first thing stripped: one screenshot later it is a picture of a flooded street with your masthead on it, and within one repost it is evidence that a street flooded.

2 · An inbound photograph carries no Content Credentials at all. What does that tell you?

Provenance is asymmetric. A valid credential chain is strong evidence; a missing one is the overwhelming default and simply sends you back to the other four checks. Signing your own output is how that base rate improves, and it is the part you control.

3 · A reader submits an image as their own photograph and your metadata check says otherwise. What do you send back?

Describe, do not diagnose. The original resolves it in a minute if they are genuine, the wording accuses nobody, and it keeps a contributor you need. A detector score is unanswerable and unexplainable, and silence loses the person without teaching them anything.
