Audio: transcription, editing, podcast

You have 50 minutes of interview with the port's operations director sitting on a drive, a newsletter going out in the morning and a podcast on Thursday. Machine transcription will hand you the full text of that interview in about the time it takes to make coffee, and it will be wrong in specific, predictable places: the names, the numbers, the technical terms, the accents, the crosstalk. Which is to say, wrong in exactly the places a contested story turns on. Tonight you get the four high-risk zones and the listen-back habit that covers them, the edit line an audio cut may never cross, the reverse skill of spotting a splice in a file someone sends you, and the podcast workflow with AI in the safe places and out of the unsafe ones.

Part 0

Where this sits in the chain

The publish chain from b1 runs Tip → Verify → Report → Package → Publish, and stage 2 is a gate rather than a step. This session and the next are Package. That sounds like the safe end of the chain, and it is not. Packaging is where a verified fact quietly turns into a claim the speaker never made: a transcript puts a sentence in the wrong mouth, an edit removes the qualifier that was the entire story, a clip answers a question nobody asked. The trust line - the claim, the attribution, the correction - is easiest to break here, because nothing looks like it is being invented. Everything in tonight's audio is real. The failure is in what got joined, trimmed or relabelled on the way out.

Part 1 · what a transcript actually is

A transcript is not a record of what was said 6 min live

A machine transcript is a best guess at the audio, rendered in the confident typography of a document. That last part is the problem. Nothing on the page tells you which words the model was sure about and which it invented to fill a gap, so a 3% error rate does not feel like a 3% error rate, it feels like a finished text. And the errors are not scattered evenly. They cluster on proper nouns, on figures and units, on domain vocabulary, on accented speech, and on any moment where two people talk over each other. Those are the same passages you will want to quote.

LiveDiarisation: the most dangerous transcript failure there is4 min▶

Speaker diarisation is the model's guess at who is talking, and it is a separate guess from the words. That separation is why it is so dangerous. A word error looks like a word error: "birthing plan" stops you, because it is nonsense. A diarisation error produces a perfectly fluent, perfectly plausible sentence attached to the wrong human being.

  • It fails where speakers overlap, and on similar voices. Interruptions and agreements force the model to cut the stream somewhere, and it cuts it wrong, or it collapses two people of similar register into one label.
  • It fails at the top of the file. Labels are assigned before the model has heard much of anyone, so the first two minutes are the least reliable and are often where the framing is set.
  • It fails silently. There is no confidence marker on a speaker tag. SPEAKER 1 looks exactly as certain on line 400 as on line 4.

The consequence is the worst outcome on the trust line: an accurate quote, correctly transcribed, attributed to a person who did not say it. You cannot correct that quietly. It is not a typo, it is a false attribution, and the person it lands on is entitled to be furious about it.

The one habit that covers all four zones Before any passage goes in a script, a newsletter or a show, open the audio at that timecode and listen to it. Not the sentence, the thirty seconds around it. This takes about forty seconds per quote and it catches names, numbers, terms and speaker swaps in one pass, because you are checking against the thing that actually happened rather than against a text that already looks finished.
LiveWhat a transcript is genuinely good for3 min▶

None of this means do not transcribe. Transcription is one of the strongest things AI does for a newsroom, and refusing it costs far more than it saves. It is excellent for navigation (finding the four minutes in fifty that matter, so you listen to the right part properly rather than the whole thing badly), search across a body of audio (six interviews on the port expansion become searchable text, which is genuinely new capability and is what b8 is built on), sharing with a desk, and accessibility, which Part 3 comes back to.

The line is between using a transcript to find things and using a transcript as the record. The first is what it is for. The second is what gets corrected. Three habits keep you on the right side of it: never strip the timecodes, because a quote you cannot listen back to should not be published; tag anything you have not heard yourself as [CHECK AUDIO] and clear the tags by hand; and keep the raw machine output alongside your corrected version, because if a dispute arises you want both, plus the original recording, which is the only actual evidence.

Part 2 · the rule you write tonight

The edit line 6 min live

Every audio desk edits. Removing an "um" is not deception, it is courtesy to the listener, and a newsroom that refuses to tighten anything is not being more honest, it is being less listenable. The line is not between edited and unedited. It is between edits that change how something sounds and edits that change what was said. That line is crossable by accident, at speed, with good intentions, which is why it needs to be written down on your desk rather than held in someone's head.

An audio edit mayAn audio edit may never
Remove filler - ums, ahs, false startsJoin two answers, or two calls, into one
Tighten a long pause without deleting itRemove a qualifier, a unit or a hedge
Cut a whole digression that stands aloneChange the question a clip appears to answer
Fix level, hum, clipping and room noiseMake a hesitation sound like certainty
Trim the top and tail of an answerReorder anything, at any scale, ever
LiveThe reverse skill: spotting a splice in audio someone sends you5 min▶

The item from the b1 desk: a piece of audio circulating online in which a chief executive appears to admit price fixing. It is not synthetic. There is no generated voice in it, nothing a detector could ever have flagged. It is two real sentences, spoken by the real person, taken from two different calls and joined. Every individual word is authentic. The admission is fabricated.

  • Room tone shifts. Every space has a noise floor. A join between two recordings steps between two different floors, and the step is audible at the splice point once you know to listen for it.
  • Breath that does not continue. People breathe in patterns across a sentence. A splice interrupts the pattern: a phrase starting without the in-breath that should precede it, or an in-breath leading nowhere.
  • Impossible cadence, and codec seams. Two clauses at slightly different speaking rates or pitch centres, welded at a plausible comma. Two phone calls also compress differently, so hiss and handset colouration change at the join.
  • No question anywhere. An admission with no audible prompt around it is a shape that almost never occurs naturally. Ask what was asked.

And the ordinary journalism, which matters more than any of the above: ask for the full recording. A source with a genuine clip usually has the call it came from. A source who cannot produce the surrounding audio has told you something important.

Real world

This is the item on the b1 desk that only the expert call caught. Reverse search returns nothing, because the audio is not a reused file. Metadata and C2PA return nothing, because a re-encoded audio file carries no useful history. An AI detector returns nothing useful, because nothing was generated. What caught it was five minutes with someone who knew the company and the calls, who said the two sentences came from meetings months apart. The cheapest check on the list, and the only one that worked.

LiveSynthetic voice: consent from the person, disclosure to the listener4 min▶

Voice cloning and text-to-speech have real newsroom uses, and pretending otherwise loses you accessibility and multilingual reach for no benefit. The uses that hold up: a translated read of a piece published in another language; a voiced correction or a voiced article, turning text you already published into audio; accessibility reads of newsletters; and continuity for a presenter who has consented in writing to a cloned voice within named limits.

Two requirements sit on top of all of these, and neither is negotiable:

  • Consent from the person whose voice it is. Specific, written and scoped: what it may be used for, for how long, and how it is revoked. A voice is not newsroom property because that person once recorded for you, and a staff contract is not consent to be cloned.
  • Disclosure to the listener. Said in the audio, not only written in the show notes, because the show notes are not what the listener is hearing. "This translation is read by a synthetic voice" costs three seconds.

And the hard prohibition: never synthesise a real person saying something they did not say. Not for illustration, not for a reconstruction, not with a label, because the label does not survive a repost and the audio does. Dramatised readings remain fine under the old convention - a clearly different voice, an explicit "read by an actor", verbatim words from a document you hold. b7 covers the equivalent rule for images and takes illustration versus depiction all the way through.

Part 3 · the weekly show, end to end

Where AI belongs in the podcast week

The Harbour Ledger's podcast is one producer, one presenter and whoever on the desk has something to say that week. The realistic question is not whether to use AI in that workflow, it is which stages it may touch. The sorting rule is the one from b1: automate the preparation, never the assertion.

LiveThe workflow, stage by stage, with the line drawn4 min▶

Safe - AI prepares, you check:

  • Rough transcript of every recording, immediately, timecodes intact. The highest-value item in the whole week.
  • Chapter marks proposed from the transcript. You rename them, because a chapter title is an editorial claim about what a segment contains.
  • Show notes draft from the transcript, then rewritten by a person, with every name, number and link verified against the audio rather than against the draft.
  • Clip candidates with timecodes and full surrounding context, plus levelling, de-essing and noise reduction, where no meaning is at stake.

Unsafe - a person owns this or it does not happen:

  • Choosing the clip. A clip is an editorial claim about what the interview was, which is the whole of b6 Part 1.
  • Making the cut. Anything that joins, trims or reorders speech is the edit line, and the edit line is human.
  • Writing what someone said. Show notes that paraphrase a guest into a sharper sentence than they used are a misquote with better copywriting.
  • Any voice that is not the person's own, and publishing itself. Someone's name is on it, and that is the trust line.
LiveCorrecting in audio, where nobody re-listens4 min▶

Text corrections work because the corrected page replaces the wrong one at the same address. Audio has no such mechanism. Somebody downloaded Thursday's episode on Thursday, and they are never going to hear whatever you say about it the following Thursday. Plan for that:

  • Correct at the top of the next episode. Not at the end, where the audience has already gone. Read it plainly and briefly.
  • Correct in the show notes of the wrong episode. Notes are usually editable in place even when the audio is not, and this is the one thing a late listener will actually see.
  • Re-cut and re-upload for anything serious. A false attribution or a wrong figure at the centre of a story justifies replacing the file, with a note saying it was replaced and why.
  • Correct the transcript too, and never replace an episode silently. The transcript is the searchable record, and a file that changes without a note is the audio equivalent of stealth-editing a story.
LiveAccessibility: the genuine, unambiguous win3 min▶

Most of this session is about restraint. This card is not. Transcripts for every piece of audio you publish used to be a budget line most desks could not carry, and now they are close to free. That is a real expansion of who can use your journalism.

  • Publish a transcript with every episode, corrected in the four high-risk zones and labelled honestly - "auto-generated, lightly edited" is fair, presenting an unchecked machine transcript as the record is not.
  • It is also an archive and a search asset. Published transcripts make years of audio findable, which is b8's territory, and they are how audio journalism shows up in search and answer systems at all. Fix the transcript whenever you correct the audio: two records of one episode that disagree is worse than one.
Self-studyArchival audio and the rights that come with it3 min read▶

The moment transcription is cheap, someone suggests running the whole tape library through it, and they are right to. The questions that arrive with it are rights questions rather than technical ones:

  • What did the contributor agree to? An interview recorded in 1998 for a radio programme was probably not licensed for indefinite podcast reuse, and definitely not for voice cloning.
  • Who owns the recording? Freelance, co-production and syndicated material frequently is not yours to republish, whatever is on the shelf. Archive audio is also full of cleared-at-the-time music whose licences have long since expired.
  • People who are now private, or dead. Findability changes the ethics. Material nobody could locate in 1998 is a different object once it is fully searchable.

b8 Archives and search takes all of these properly, including how to make decades of tape findable without publishing anything you do not have the right to publish.

Build-along · 25 min

Cut the 50-minute interview ★ everyone runs it

You have the operations director on tape for 50 minutes. You need three things by tomorrow: a two-minute podcast segment, one social clip, and two quotes for the newsletter. Run it in this order, and notice that the transcript is used to find and never to quote.

Transcribe the full 50 minutes with timecodes. Do not correct anything yet. Read it once, fast, purely to find where the story is.

Confirm speaker labels at the top of the file by ear. The first two minutes are the least reliable, and every label downstream inherits them.

Run the good prompt below for a clip shortlist. You get candidates with full context, not cuts.

Open the audio at each candidate timecode and listen to the surrounding minute. Kill any candidate whose meaning depends on what you would cut.

Make the cut yourself: ums out, pause tightened not deleted, qualifier untouched, nothing joined, nothing reordered.

For the two newsletter quotes, listen back one final time and check the four zones: name, number, term, speaker.

Now run the bad prompt on the same file and compare its output line by line against the transcript. Find the join.

✅ Good - produces candidates, structurally cannot produce a cut 
You have the timecoded transcript of a 50-minute interview with the port
operations director, Harbour Ledger, port expansion story.

Produce a CLIP SHORTLIST only. For each candidate give me:
  - start and end timecode, to the second
  - the verbatim transcript text of the clip
  - the 60 seconds before and the 60 seconds after, verbatim
  - the question the clip is answering, quoted in full
  - one line: what a listener would NOT know from the clip alone

Hard constraints:
  - Do NOT edit, trim, tighten, paraphrase or clean up any text.
  - Do NOT join material from different timecodes into one quote.
  - Do NOT remove qualifiers, hedges, numbers or units.
  - Do NOT alter or infer speaker labels; report them as given.
  - Mark anything unclear as [CHECK AUDIO] rather than guessing.

Return 6 candidates ranked by newsworthiness, and nothing else.
❌ Bad - silently joins and trims, and hands you the result as finished work 
Pull the best quotes from this interview and tighten them for broadcast.
Keep it punchy - drop the ums, cut the waffle, and give me clean
20-second clips I can drop straight into the show.
What the bad prompt actually did "Tightened for broadcast" is an instruction to change the words, and the model will follow it. In practice it drops the qualifier, because the qualifier is the least punchy part of the sentence, and it welds the strongest clause of one answer to the strongest clause of another because that reads better than either alone. You get a quote that is fluent, quotable and never said. The transcript will not flag it, because a polished summary is what you were handed, and you have no timecodes to check it against.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-40 min total

  • Transcribe a real interview you have already published from. Listen back to every passage you quoted, and count the errors by zone: names, numbers, technical terms, crosstalk. Most people find at least one in a twenty-minute file.
  • Check the speaker labels in the first two minutes of that file specifically. If they are wrong there, work out how far the error propagated.
  • Write your desk's one-line rule for what an audio edit may never do, in words a freelancer could follow on their first shift. Put it where the edit happens, not in a policy document.
  • Take one clip you have published and ask the test question honestly: would the speaker recognise their own meaning in it?
  • Run both prompts from the build-along on the same file and diff the outputs. Keep the diff - it is the most persuasive thing you can show an editor who wants clips faster.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Which transcript error is the most dangerous, and why?

A word error stops you, because it reads as nonsense and someone investigates. A diarisation error produces a perfectly plausible sentence in the wrong mouth, with no confidence marker and nothing to catch the eye. That is a false attribution rather than a typo, and it cannot be corrected quietly.

2 · Your editor wants a tighter clip. Which of these crosses the edit line?

Filler removal and pause tightening change how something sounds. Joining two answers changes what was said: it manufactures a sentence the speaker never uttered, out of words they genuinely used. That is the exact mechanism behind the price-fixing clip, where every individual word is authentic and the admission is fabricated.

3 · You want to publish a translated read of an article in a synthetic voice. What is required?

Both requirements stand together. A voice does not become newsroom property because someone once recorded for you, and a note buried in the show notes is not disclosure, because show notes are not what the listener is hearing. The hard prohibition sits above both: never synthesise a real person saying something they did not say.
